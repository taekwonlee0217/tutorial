## 1. The philosophical framework

### A. Psychology, freedom, and sin

1. {Why does Kierkegaard’s introduction distinguish the possibility of sin from actual sin?} : {Psychology can investigate how wrongdoing becomes possible, but treating that possibility as an inevitable cause would erase the freedom through which an individual actually becomes guilty.}
    
2. {Why does Kierkegaard object to explaining wrongdoing as merely a psychological condition?} : {Such an explanation turns a responsible act into an observable state, whereas wrongdoing requires the individual’s ethical acknowledgment rather than detached observation alone.}
    
3. {What does Kierkegaard’s criticism of logical transitions establish about freedom?} : {A logical account of how concepts relate cannot establish why an existing individual freely turns a possibility into an actual deed.}
    
4. {Why does the introduction connect anxiety with the Christian doctrine of hereditary sin?} : {Kierkegaard investigates the psychological possibility of wrongdoing while preserving the distinction between humanity’s inherited sinful situation and each individual’s responsibility for actual sin.}  
    [Source: _The Concept of Dread_, Introduction](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/philosophy/works/dk/kierkega.htm)
    

## 2. “Anxiety is the dizziness of freedom”

### A. The person looking into an abyss

5. {What does Kierkegaard’s example of looking into a yawning abyss illustrate?} : {The person becomes dizzy through the relationship between the depth and their own looking, just as anxiety arises when freedom encounters its own possibilities.}
    
6. {Why does Kierkegaard locate dizziness partly in the observer’s eye?} : {The abyss alone does not explain dizziness, because the observer’s engagement with it helps produce the experience, making the image suitable for freedom’s relationship with itself.}
    
7. {What does “anxiety is the dizziness of freedom” mean in this example?} : {Freedom becomes unsettled when it confronts what it can bring about, because possibility opens before it without determining the action it will take.}
    

### B. Freedom becoming entangled

8. {Why does freedom grasp finiteness for support in the abyss passage?} : {Confronting its own possibility overwhelms freedom, which seeks support in finite conditions rather than sustaining itself confidently in that openness.}
    
9. {What does Kierkegaard’s description of anxiety as “entangled freedom” explain?} : {Freedom becomes caught in its own anxious relationship with possibility, yet this entanglement neither necessitates wrongdoing nor excuses the eventual act.}  
    [Source: Kierkegaard’s abyss and entangled-freedom passages](https://sites.google.com/view/philosophy-texts/19th-century/kierkegaard/kierkegaard-text-pieces)
    

## 3. Innocence and awakening spirit

### A. Anxiety before guilt

10. {What does “innocence is ignorance” mean in Kierkegaard’s account?} : {Innocence lacks the developed distinction between good and evil, rather than possessing moral knowledge and deliberately choosing goodness.}
    
11. {What does Kierkegaard mean by spirit dreaming in innocence?} : {The human capacity for spiritual selfhood is present but has not yet become an explicitly established relationship to itself.}
    
12. {Why does the “nothing” of innocence produce anxiety?} : {Spirit intimates a possibility that innocence cannot yet identify, so anxiety concerns an indeterminate something rather than a definite threatening object.}
    

### B. Children and fascination

13. {What do children seeking adventure and mysterious experiences reveal about anxiety?} : {Their simultaneous attraction and apprehension show that anxiety can accompany innocent fascination without already constituting guilt.}
    
14. {How does “sympathetic antipathy” describe this attraction?} : {Anxiety draws someone toward the very possibility that repels them, combining fascination and recoil rather than simple desire or straightforward fear.}  
    [Source: _The Concept of Anxiety_, chapter I, selections](https://www.thetedkarchive.com/library/howard-v-hong-and-edna-h-hong-the-essential-kierkegaard)
    

## 4. Adam and the forbidden fruit

### A. The prohibition awakens possibility

15. {What happens when Adam hears the prohibition against eating the fruit?} : {The prohibition awakens awareness that he can act, bringing freedom’s possibility into view before he understands the distinction between good and evil.}
    
16. {Why is Adam’s anxiety not initially a knowledgeable choice between good and evil?} : {The moral distinction associated with eating the fruit is not yet available to innocent Adam, whose anxiety concerns his capacity to act.}
    
17. {What does the “anxious possibility of being able” identify in Adam?} : {Adam encounters his own agency as an unsettled possibility, without possessing a complete understanding of what exercising it will mean.}
    

### B. Adam and every individual

18. {Why does Kierkegaard’s interpretation of Adam concern subsequent human beings?} : {Adam illustrates the encounter between freedom and possibility that each individual undergoes, so his story cannot replace anyone else’s personal responsibility.}  
    [Textual anchor: Adam passages quoted by the Hong Kierkegaard Library](https://wp.stolaf.edu/kierkegaard/anxiety-in-the-garden-an-introduction-to-existential-angst-and-its-relationship-with-choice/)

## 5. The leap, embodiment, and human history

### A. Becoming guilty

19. {What does the qualitative leap in Adam’s fall mean?} : {Actual wrongdoing introduces a new condition of guilt that cannot be produced simply by accumulating anxiety or intensifying an innocent possibility.}
    
20. {Why can inherited sinfulness not make an individual’s first sin necessary?} : {Historical conditions can increase the individual’s predisposition toward wrongdoing, but guilt still enters through that individual’s own qualitative leap.}
    

### B. Body, shame, and creation

21. {Why does Kierkegaard distinguish sensuousness from sinfulness?} : {Bodily existence and sexuality do not themselves constitute wrongdoing, which depends on the individual’s spiritual and ethical relationship to them.}
    
22. {What does Kierkegaard’s discussion of bashfulness or shame show?} : {Anxiety can accompany an emerging awareness of sexual difference before that awareness becomes a definite sexual desire.}
    
23. {What does the biblical image of creation’s anxious expectation illustrate?} : {Kierkegaard calls the reflection of humanity’s sinfulness throughout creation objective anxiety, distinguishing it from an individual’s subjective experience.}  
    [Source: _The Concept of Dread_, chapters I–II](https://archive.org/stream/TheConceptOfDread/TheConceptOfDread_djvu.txt)
    

## 6. Time and the future

### A. Possibility lies ahead

24. {Why does Kierkegaard connect anxiety with the future?} : {The future is the field of what may happen, making it the temporal expression of the possibilities toward which anxiety turns.}
    
25. {How can anxiety concern something that happened in the past?} : {The past becomes an object of anxiety insofar as it may recur or acquire consequences that remain possible in the future.}
    

### B. The moment and eternity

26. {What does Kierkegaard’s account of the moment add to his analysis?} : {The moment marks the intersection of temporal existence and eternity, allowing human life to possess spiritual significance beyond mere succession.}
    
27. {What does his example of a road without the walker’s step explain?} : {Without the decisive moment, time appears chiefly as accumulated distance behind someone, obscuring the living relationship between past, future, and eternity.}  
    [Source: _The Concept of Anxiety_, excerpt on time and the moment](https://www.sweetstudy.com/files/kierkegaard-theconceptofanxiety.pdf)
    

## 7. Anxiety after wrongdoing

### A. Anxiety about evil

28. {What does Kierkegaard’s description of anxiety about evil show about repeated wrongdoing?} : {After sin becomes actual, anxiety can concern its recurrence, creating an unfree relationship in which the individual fears wrongdoing while remaining caught in it.}

### B. Anxiety about the good

29. {Why does Kierkegaard use Gospel encounters with possessed people to illustrate anxiety about the good?} : {Their resistance to Christ represents an unfree condition that experiences approaching liberation as a threat.}
    
30. {What does Kierkegaard’s account of self-enclosure explain about this resistance?} : {The individual attempts to preserve an unfree condition by shutting out the disclosure and communication through which freedom could become possible.}
    
31. {Why is the possibility of disclosure frightening in this self-enclosed condition?} : {Disclosure threatens the individual’s defended isolation, so the good becomes alarming precisely because it could interrupt that unfreedom.}  
    [Sources: Kierkegaard passages on anxiety about evil](https://philarchive.org/archive/VENSMI) and [self-enclosure and anxiety about the good](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21692327.2019.1649602)
    

## 8. Education through possibility

### A. Possibility includes catastrophe

32. {Why does Kierkegaard call possibility the weightiest category?} : {Possibility includes devastating outcomes alongside desirable ones, confronting the individual with uncertainties that exceed any single actual circumstance.}
    
33. {Why does he criticize people who imagine possibility only as happiness or good fortune?} : {They substitute comforting fantasy for genuine possibility, which also includes loss, destruction, and everything that unsettles their expectations.}
    

### B. Learning to understand actuality

34. {What does graduating from the school of possibility teach?} : {The individual learns that life guarantees neither preferred outcomes nor exemption from suffering, undermining the assumption that security is something they can demand.}
    
35. {Why can actuality appear lighter after this education?} : {An actual difficulty has definite boundaries, whereas imagined possibility can expose someone to an unrestricted range of terrible outcomes.}  
    [Textual anchor: Kierkegaard’s chapter V passage on education by possibility](https://samirchopra.com/2020/08/28/kierkegaard-on-being-educated-by-possibility-and-anxiety/)
    

## 9. Learning anxiety through faith

### A. Passing through possibility

36. {What does Kierkegaard mean by passing through anxiety rather than escaping it?} : {The individual allows possibility to challenge finite assurances instead of treating avoidance of uncertainty as the foundation of freedom.}
    
37. {Why does this education change someone’s relationship to ordinary dangers?} : {Having faced the greater terrors of possibility, the individual can encounter particular difficulties without granting them absolute power over their existence.}  
    [Textual anchor: Kierkegaard’s passage on passing through anxiety, reproduced in the University of Alabama study](https://ir-api.ua.edu/api/core/bitstreams/6ad04cc4-7dbd-42db-9154-6230d5b6a77f/content)
    

### B. Faith and reconciliation

38. {Why does Kierkegaard say anxiety becomes fully educative through faith?} : {Faith enables the individual to undergo possibility’s destruction of finite assurances without making that destruction the final truth about existence.}
    
39. {What does Kierkegaard’s account of education by anxiety require beyond optimistic expectation?} : {The individual must face possibility honestly, including its terrifying dimensions, while relating to something deeper than hoped-for worldly outcomes.}
    
40. {Why does Kierkegaard’s discussion of guilt conclude with atonement?} : {Recognizing guilt exposes the limits of self-sufficiency, directing the individual toward Christian reconciliation rather than an assurance of innocence achieved through reflection alone.}  
    [Textual anchor: Kierkegaard’s chapter V passages on faith and atonement](https://ir-api.ua.edu/api/core/bitstreams/6ad04cc4-7dbd-42db-9154-6230d5b6a77f/content)

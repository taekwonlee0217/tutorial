## Topic 1: The disappearance of storytelling

### A. Distance and recognition — §I

1. {Why does Benjamin compare recognizing the storyteller in Leskov to seeing a human or animal shape in a rock?} : {The comparison suggests that historical distance makes storytelling’s defining features visible, just as a particular viewing position reveals a recognizable shape in a rock.}
    
2. {Why does presenting Leskov as a storyteller increase our distance from him?} : {It reveals his connection to forms of shared experience and communication that Benjamin believes have become increasingly unfamiliar to modern readers.}
    
3. {What does the embarrassment that arises when someone requests a story reveal?} : {It indicates that exchanging experience through narration has ceased to be an ordinary social ability, even though people once treated that ability as a secure human possession.}
    

### B. War and the loss of communicable experience — §I

4. {Why does Benjamin emphasize the silence of soldiers returning from the First World War?} : {Their silence demonstrates that undergoing extraordinary events does not necessarily produce experience that people can communicate meaningfully to others.}
    
5. {Why does the later abundance of war books fail to disprove Benjamin’s account of returning soldiers’ silence?} : {Publishing accounts of war does not itself restore the shared oral transmission through which experience becomes available as guidance within a community.}
    
6. {Why does Benjamin bring together trench warfare, inflation, mechanical destruction, and political power?} : {Together they show established strategic, economic, bodily, and moral expectations being overturned, weakening the inherited frameworks through which experience previously made sense.}
    
7. {What is the significance of the generation that traveled to school by horse-drawn transport and later faced a devastated battlefield?} : {The contrast exposes the speed of historical transformation and the vulnerability of the human body within a world whose familiar conditions have disappeared.}
    

## Topic 2: The sources and communities of storytelling

### A. Oral tradition and two storyteller types — §II

8. {Why does Benjamin identify experience transmitted orally as the source of storytelling?} : {Oral transmission connects an individual occurrence to other people’s lives, allowing experience to circulate beyond its original owner.}
    
9. {Why does Benjamin value written stories that remain close to the speech of anonymous storytellers?} : {Their literary achievement preserves the rhythms and transmissibility of collective narration rather than severing the story from its oral origins.}
    
10. {What does the traveling seaman contribute to storytelling?} : {The seaman supplies knowledge of distant places and unfamiliar circumstances, bringing experiences beyond the listener’s immediate surroundings into the community.}
    
11. {What does the resident farmer contribute to storytelling?} : {The farmer preserves the accumulated knowledge, customs, and histories of a particular place, giving storytelling depth in time as well as geographical rootedness.}
    
12. {Why must the seaman and farmer be understood together?} : {Their complementary contributions show that storytelling draws both on encounters with unfamiliar worlds and on sustained knowledge of an inherited local world.}
    

### B. The workshop as a meeting place — §II

13. {Why does Benjamin give medieval workshops a central place in storytelling’s history?} : {They brought settled masters and traveling journeymen together, allowing local memories and knowledge acquired elsewhere to enter a common exchange.}
    
14. {Why does it matter that a settled master had previously been a traveling journeyman?} : {The master’s own life already combined mobility and rootedness, showing that Benjamin’s two storyteller types overlap rather than form exclusive categories.}
    

## Topic 3: Leskov’s experience and practical orientation

### A. Travel, religion, and ordinary life — §III

15. {How did Leskov’s commercial journeys through Russia contribute to his storytelling?} : {They exposed him to varied social conditions and religious communities, providing concrete knowledge of people and ways of living that informed his narratives.}
    
16. {Why does Benjamin distinguish Leskov’s religious interests from his opposition to Orthodox bureaucracy?} : {The distinction shows that religious commitment can support criticism of institutions when living goodness matters more than official authority.}
    
17. {Why are Leskov’s righteous characters usually active ordinary people rather than ascetics?} : {Their goodness takes shape through conduct within everyday life, locating wisdom in practical relationships rather than withdrawal from the world.}
    
18. {Why does Benjamin mention Leskov’s early writings about book prices, workers, drinking, doctors, and unemployment?} : {These subjects establish his attention to concrete social problems and connect his later fiction to an already developed concern with ordinary life.}
    

### B. Usefulness and counsel — §IV

19. {Why does Benjamin mention Gotthelf’s agricultural advice, Nodier’s concern with gas lighting, and Hebel’s scientific instruction?} : {These examples demonstrate that storytelling’s usefulness can include practical knowledge and education as well as explicitly moral guidance.}
    
20. {Why does Benjamin include morals, practical instructions, proverbs, and rules of life among storytelling’s possible benefits?} : {Their variety shows that a story can guide its listeners in several ways without being reducible to a single kind of lesson.}
    
21. {Why does Benjamin define counsel through the continuation of an unfolding story?} : {Counsel responds to a person’s developing circumstances by suggesting how life might proceed, rather than supplying an answer detached from that person’s situation.}
    
22. {Why must someone seeking counsel first be able to tell their situation?} : {Narrating the situation makes its circumstances and possibilities available to another person, enabling advice to address the actual life being lived.}
    
23. {How does Benjamin connect wisdom to counsel embedded in lived experience?} : {Wisdom emerges when guidance has been tested and shaped within life, giving it a practical authority that abstract information alone cannot provide.}
    

### C. Historical change — §IV

24. {Why does Benjamin resist treating storytelling’s disappearance as merely a symptom of cultural decay?} : {He understands it as part of a long transformation in social production and communication, which also makes the beauty of the disappearing practice newly perceptible.}

## Topic 4: Storytelling and the novel

### A. Different foundations — §V

25. {Why is the novel’s dependence on the book significant for Benjamin?} : {It ties the novel to a mode of composition and reception whose existence does not depend on the oral circulation that sustains traditional storytelling.}
    
26. {Why does Benjamin distinguish the storyteller’s use of experience from the novelist’s solitude?} : {The storyteller participates in an exchange of personal and reported experience, whereas the novelist begins from an individual whose deepest concerns no longer readily become shared counsel.}
    
27. {Why does the fullness of life represented in a novel reveal perplexity rather than necessarily produce wisdom?} : {Accumulating the details of a life does not guarantee that those details can be organized into guidance that another person can use.}
    

### B. Particular kinds of novels — §V

28. {Why does Benjamin use Don Quixote to distinguish nobility from wisdom?} : {Don Quixote’s generosity and courage coexist with a lack of effective counsel, demonstrating that admirable character does not automatically yield practical understanding.}
    
29. {Why does Benjamin treat the instruction in Goethe’s _Wilhelm Meister’s Journeyman Years_ as a modification of the novel form?} : {Introducing transmissible guidance changes a form that Benjamin otherwise associates with individual perplexity and the absence of shared counsel.}
    
30. {Why does Benjamin criticize the Bildungsroman’s integration of personal development and social order?} : {Presenting an individual’s formation as accommodation to society offers a fragile justification of social arrangements whose actual conditions may contradict that reconciliation.}
    

## Topic 5: Information and the press

### A. Historical emergence — §VI

31. {Why does Benjamin compare changes in narrative forms to changes in the earth’s surface?} : {The comparison emphasizes that forms of communication emerge and disappear through long historical processes rather than through a single sudden literary event.}
    
32. {Why does Benjamin connect the influence of information to the press and bourgeois society?} : {Their development establishes institutions and audiences that favor promptly accessible accounts of current events, altering the conditions under which narratives receive attention.}
    
33. {Why is information a threat to both storytelling and the novel?} : {Its demand for immediate relevance and explanation competes with the slower, differently organized experiences that these narrative forms offer.}
    

### B. Proximity, plausibility, and explanation — §VI

34. {What does Villemessant’s preference for a nearby attic fire over a revolution in Madrid illustrate?} : {It shows that newsworthiness can depend on a reader’s immediate proximity and practical interest rather than on an event’s wider historical importance.}
    
35. {Why does Benjamin contrast information’s prompt verifiability with the authority of reports from distant places or earlier times?} : {The contrast identifies different grounds of credibility, with information asking for immediate confirmation and traditional reports drawing authority from distance and transmission.}
    
36. {Why does Benjamin say information must sound plausible without necessarily being more accurate?} : {Plausibility concerns whether an account fits the audience’s expectations, which does not by itself establish that the account is true.}
    
37. {Why can receiving abundant news leave people poor in memorable stories?} : {An event packaged with a ready explanation can limit the listener’s further engagement instead of opening possibilities for reflection and retelling.}
    
38. {Why does Benjamin praise Leskov’s restraint in explaining events in works such as _The Deception_ and _The White Eagle_?} : {Leaving psychological connections unstated allows readers to interpret the narrated events through their own experience.}
    

## Topic 6: Interpretation and a story’s lasting power

### A. Psammenitus — §VII

39. {Why is Psammenitus’s grief at the sight of his servant more puzzling than an ordinary account of mourning?} : {His earlier restraint before his children’s suffering makes the later reaction resist an immediately obvious explanation.}
    
40. {Why does Benjamin present Montaigne’s interpretation alongside other possible interpretations of Psammenitus’s reaction?} : {The alternatives demonstrate that an unexplained narrative can continue generating thought without being exhausted by one definitive psychological account.}
    
41. {Why does Benjamin compare the story’s endurance to ancient seeds retaining their capacity to germinate?} : {The image suggests that a narrative can preserve possibilities of meaning that become active again when encountered by later listeners.}
    

### B. Story versus information — §§VI–VII

42. {How does the continuing interpretability of the Psammenitus story distinguish it from information?} : {Its significance can develop through later encounters, whereas information’s principal value depends on the moment when it delivers something new.}

## Topic 7: Listening, boredom, and memory

### A. Conditions of absorption — §VIII

43. {Why does Benjamin connect narrative compactness with the listener’s memory?} : {An account unburdened by extensive psychological explanation is easier to retain, integrate into experience, and reproduce for another person.}
    
44. {What does Benjamin’s image of boredom as a bird incubating experience suggest?} : {Unhurried mental receptiveness gives what has been heard time to settle into experience instead of being displaced immediately by another stimulus.}
    
45. {Why does Benjamin associate weaving and spinning with effective listening?} : {Their familiar rhythms can reduce self-conscious distraction, enabling listeners to absorb a story while their hands continue working.}
    

### B. The community of retelling — §VIII

46. {Why does storytelling depend on listeners becoming retellers?} : {A story survives as a shared practice only when its recipients retain it and carry it into subsequent encounters.}
    
47. {Why does the disappearance of traditional listening activities threaten an entire community?} : {It weakens the recurring situations in which people hear, remember, and exchange narratives, disrupting the social continuity of storytelling.}
    

## Topic 8: Storytelling as craftsmanship

### A. The storyteller’s trace — §IX

48. {Why does Benjamin compare a storyteller’s presence in a narrative to a potter’s handprints on a vessel?} : {The comparison presents narration as material shaped by a particular person’s life and handling, with that shaping remaining perceptible in the finished work.}
    
49. {Why do Leskov’s accounts of train journeys, funerals, and reading gatherings matter as narrative openings?} : {They locate a story within an encounter through which it was received, preserving its connection to a chain of people and circumstances.}
    
50. {Why does Benjamin invoke Leskov’s understanding of writing as a craft?} : {It supports the view that narration involves sustained practical skill in shaping experience, comparable to an artisan’s handling of materials.}
    

### B. Labor and accumulated time — §IX

51. {Why does Benjamin discuss the craftsmen of Tula in connection with _The Steel Flea_?} : {Their extraordinary workmanship exemplifies the value Leskov assigns to artisanal skill within a world increasingly organized by technological and economic progress.}
    
52. {Why does Benjamin use Valéry’s examples of pearls, mature wine, carvings, and layered artwork?} : {They illustrate forms of completion that depend on patient accumulation, helping explain how repeated telling can gradually shape a narrative.}
    
53. {Why does Benjamin connect modern impatience with the development of the short story?} : {He sees the pressure to abbreviate as weakening the slow accumulation of retellings through which traditional narratives acquired depth and finish.}
    

## Topic 9: Death and narrative authority

### A. Death’s changing visibility — §X

54. {Why does Benjamin contrast medieval deathbed scenes with modern hospitals and sanatoria?} : {The contrast shows dying moving away from shared social visibility, reducing occasions when a person’s completed life becomes available to others as experience.}
    
55. {Why does Benjamin connect the declining idea of eternity with resistance to sustained effort?} : {When enduring time loses its imaginative force, practices requiring patience and continuity become harder to sustain within everyday life.}
    

### B. The authority of a completed life — §§X–XI

56. {Why does even an otherwise insignificant dying person possess authority in Benjamin’s account?} : {The approaching completion of a life gives its expressions and remembered experiences a finality that others can recognize and transmit.}
    
57. {Why does Benjamin connect the storyteller’s authority to death?} : {Mortality gives human experience its weight and limits, allowing narrated lives to carry significance beyond the immediate circumstances of their telling.}
    

## Topic 10: Natural history and historical narration

### A. Hebel’s _Unexpected Reunion_ — §XI

58. {Why does the reunion between the aged bride and the preserved body of her young fiancé matter?} : {Their unequal exposure to time makes mortality, fidelity, and material preservation converge in one striking encounter.}
    
59. {Why does Hebel place wars, political changes, deaths, and ordinary labor between the miner’s disappearance and rediscovery?} : {The sequence embeds an individual fate within the broader passage of history while showing everyday work continuing through extraordinary upheavals.}
    
60. {How does _Unexpected Reunion_ connect human history with natural history?} : {Its events bring political chronology together with aging, death, and the body’s material preservation, making historical life inseparable from natural processes.}
    

### B. Historian and chronicler — §XII

61. {Why does Benjamin compare historiography and epic forms to white light and the colors of a spectrum?} : {The analogy proposes a common historical ground from which different forms of narrating events emerge with distinct characteristics.}
    
62. {How does Benjamin distinguish the historian from the medieval chronicler?} : {The historian must explain relationships among events, while the chronicler situates events within a larger course of the world whose ultimate design need not be demonstrable.}
    
63. {Why does Benjamin describe the storyteller as preserving a transformed chronicler?} : {The storyteller retains the ability to embed occurrences within a larger meaningful world even when that world is no longer governed explicitly by religious history.}
    
64. {Why does _The Alexandrite_ make it difficult to separate religious and worldly interpretations of events?} : {Its connection between stones and human destiny can appear simultaneously as natural symbolism, historical significance, and a suggestion of providential order.}
    

## Topic 11: Memory and the inheritance of experience

### A. Memory as an epic capacity — §XIII

65. {Why does Benjamin invoke Mnemosyne, the Greek figure associated with memory?} : {She represents the common capacity through which events are retained and transmitted before storytelling and the novel develop different uses of remembrance.}
    
66. {Why does Benjamin mention a Leskov character’s belief in the imperial family’s extraordinary memory?} : {The belief imagines comprehensive memory at the center of the narrated world, emphasizing memory’s power to hold together a broad field of events.}
    
67. {How does remembering events enable narration to confront death?} : {Memory allows experiences to remain available after the lives and circumstances that produced them have passed away.}
    

### B. Connected stories and concentrated remembrance — §XIII

68. {Why does Benjamin use Scheherazade to explain the relationship among stories?} : {Her ability to begin another tale presents storytelling as a continuing network in which one narrative leads into others.}
    
69. {How does Benjamin distinguish the storyteller’s memory from the novelist’s remembrance?} : {Storytelling distributes attention among many connected occurrences, while the novel concentrates remembrance on the sustained significance of a particular life or undertaking.}
    
70. {Why does Benjamin mention the solemn invocations of the Muse in Homer?} : {They suggest that the focused remembrance later associated with the novel was already present within the older epic tradition.}
    

## Topic 12: The novel’s search for meaning

### A. Inheritance, loss, and time — §XIV

71. {Why does Benjamin connect Pascal’s observation about what the dead leave behind to memory?} : {He treats remembered experience as a potential inheritance whose survival depends on someone receiving and preserving it.}
    
72. {Why does the novelist’s administration of such an inheritance involve melancholy?} : {Reconstructing a life can reveal a gap between the desire for lasting meaning and the limited or unfulfilled experience that remains.}
    
73. {What does the account Benjamin draws from Lukács imply about the novel’s loss of a secure spiritual home?} : {The novel begins from a condition in which life and meaning no longer belong to an unquestioned shared order and must therefore be brought into relation through individual experience.}
    
74. {Why do hope and memory become especially important within that account of the novel?} : {Hope reaches toward unrealized possibilities while memory reorganizes the past, making both central to the individual’s struggle with time.}
    

### B. Closure and retrospective meaning — §XIV

75. {Why does Benjamin contrast the novel’s search for the meaning of life with storytelling’s moral?} : {The novel seeks an intelligible unity for a particular life, while storytelling offers guidance that can enter other people’s continuing situations.}
    
76. {Why does Benjamin discuss the remembered brothel visit at the end of Flaubert’s _Sentimental Education_?} : {The friends’ retrospective valuation of that episode gathers the disappointment and possibilities of their lives into an ending that asks the reader to interpret their overall meaning.}
    
77. {Why can a listener legitimately ask how a story continued, whereas a novel’s ending has a different function?} : {A story belongs to an extendable chain of experiences, while the novel’s closure establishes the boundary through which its represented life becomes interpretable as a whole.}
    

## Topic 13: The solitary reader and the attraction of endings

### A. Companionship and consumption — §XV

78. {Why does Benjamin say that even reading a story can preserve companionship with its teller?} : {The narrated voice maintains the sense of an experience being communicated by someone, extending the social relation associated with oral storytelling.}
    
79. {Why does Benjamin compare the novel reader’s interest to fire consuming wood?} : {The image presents reading as an intense private consumption of narrative material driven forward by suspense.}
    

### B. Death and retrospective understanding — §XV

80. {Why does Benjamin revise Moritz Heimann’s claim about a person who dies at thirty-five?} : {A life’s ending can shape how its earlier moments appear in remembrance, but those moments were not originally lived with their completed meaning already determined.}
    
81. {Why does a character’s death, or the novel’s ending, matter to the reader’s search for meaning?} : {Closure gives the represented life a bounded form that the reader can contemplate even while their own life remains unfinished.}
    
82. {What does Benjamin’s image of gaining warmth from a death encountered in reading suggest?} : {A completed fictional fate can offer emotional coherence and significance that readers struggle to obtain from their own still-developing circumstances.}
    

## Topic 14: Collective experience and the fairy tale

### A. Different traditions — §XVI

83. {Why does Benjamin place Leskov, Hebel, Poe, and Kipling within the same discussion despite their differences?} : {They demonstrate that transmitted experience can survive through religious, educational, esoteric, maritime, and military settings without taking one uniform literary form.}
    
84. {Why does Benjamin acknowledge traders’ contribution to _The Arabian Nights_?} : {Their skill in attracting attention shows that storytelling’s survival depends on techniques of engagement as well as on the usefulness of its content.}
    
85. {What does the ladder extending from underground into the clouds represent?} : {It pictures storytelling’s movement across levels of collective experience, connecting ordinary life with depths and heights beyond a single individual’s perspective.}
    

### B. Fairy tale and myth — §XVI

86. {Why does Benjamin identify the fairy-tale teller as the earliest true storyteller?} : {The fairy tale offers counsel for confronting threatening powers, making narration an early means of human orientation and liberation.}
    
87. {How does the fairy-tale fool challenge myth?} : {Apparently foolish behavior can frustrate the authority of mythical demands, showing that domination need not be confronted on its own terms.}
    
88. {Why is the successful youngest brother significant?} : {His success suggests that inherited precedence and supposedly fixed hierarchies can be overcome, opening possibilities denied by the established order.}
    
89. {What does the character who sets out to learn fear demonstrate?} : {His encounters suggest that frightening powers can be examined and understood rather than accepted as unquestionable sources of submission.}
    
90. {Why does Benjamin refer to the clever figure confronting riddles such as the Sphinx’s?} : {Intelligence can expose a supposedly overwhelming mythical challenge as a problem that human beings are capable of solving.}
    
91. {Why do animals helping a child matter to Benjamin’s account of the fairy tale?} : {They portray nature as capable of alliance with human beings instead of serving only as an instrument of threatening mythical forces.}
    
92. {Why does Benjamin connect fairy-tale courage with cunning and high spirits?} : {These complementary capacities enable people to evade domination intelligently while retaining confidence and joy in the possibility of freedom.}
    

## Topic 15: Leskov’s righteous figures and universal restoration

### A. Religion and disenchantment — §XVII

93. {Why does Benjamin bring Origen’s idea of the restoration of all souls into his discussion of Leskov?} : {It introduces a horizon of comprehensive reconciliation that helps explain Leskov’s affinity with tales of release from enchantment and suffering.}
    
94. {Why is _The Enchanted Pilgrim_ important to Benjamin’s account of resurrection?} : {It supports his reading of Leskov’s religious imagination through release from an imposed condition rather than solely through elevation into a transformed spiritual state.}
    
95. {Why does Benjamin use Ernst Bloch’s discussion of a hybrid between fairy tale and legend?} : {It helps explain how a narrative can retain legendary or mythical imagery while preserving a specifically human possibility of escape and reconciliation.}
    

### B. Maternal care and righteousness — §XVII

96. {Why does Benjamin connect Leskov’s righteous characters with the memory of his mother?} : {Her kindness provides an experiential model for characters whose wisdom appears through care for vulnerable living beings.}
    
97. {Why does Leskov’s mother’s refusal to eat animals she had known matter?} : {Her refusal extends personal recognition and responsibility beyond human relationships, treating living creatures as beings toward whom care is owed.}
    
98. {Why does Benjamin describe the righteous person as an advocate for created beings?} : {Righteousness becomes a responsibility to protect and represent the broader living world rather than an exclusively private achievement.}
    
99. {Why does Benjamin discuss the combination of male and female characteristics in _Kotin the Provider and Platonida_?} : {He reads the figure as bringing together bodily difference, maternal power, and a symbolic connection between earthly creation and divine significance.}
    
100. {Why does Benjamin acknowledge that such symbolism can endanger the fairy tale’s liberating character?} : {A symbolic figure can become fixed within mythical authority instead of sustaining the fairy tale’s movement toward human freedom.}
    
101. {Why does Benjamin contrast Leskov’s restrained righteous figures with the destructive passion of _Lady Macbeth of Mtsensk_?} : {The contrast establishes a broad range of embodied human possibilities, showing care and self-command alongside desire capable of overwhelming others.}
    

## Topic 16: Humor, wrongdoing, and practical ethics

### A. _The Voice of Nature_ — §XVIII

102. {Why does recognition through a hunting horn matter in _The Voice of Nature_?} : {The sound recalls the host’s former identity through a bodily skill, allowing recognition to arise from performance rather than explicit verbal explanation.}
    
103. {Why is the difference between the host’s reputation for honesty and his later dealings significant?} : {The discrepancy gives the recognition scene an ironic depth, preventing the character from fitting a simple moral classification.}
    

### B. Flexible judgment — §XVIII

104. {Why does Benjamin emphasize storytellers’ sympathy for rascals and crooks?} : {That sympathy acknowledges the complexity and inventiveness of lived conduct without requiring every memorable character to function as a moral exemplar.}
    
105. {Why does Benjamin describe righteousness in Hebel as a role temporarily assumed by different marginal figures?} : {It makes ethical action depend on what someone does in a particular situation rather than on their permanent social rank or reputation.}
    
106. {What does Benjamin’s characterization of Hebel as a casuist suggest?} : {Hebel judges conduct through particular cases, allowing principles to acquire value through their use in concrete circumstances.}
    
107. {Why does Benjamin connect Leskov’s practical outlook with resistance to abstract morality?} : {It locates ethical understanding in encounters with life’s actual conditions, where general rules may fail to capture the significance of an action.}
    
108. {Why does Benjamin discuss the possibility of extreme depravity turning toward saintliness?} : {The possibility challenges a fixed division between righteous and wicked people by imagining transformation at the limits of destructive passion.}
    

## Topic 17: Nature, artistic perception, and the storyteller’s final image

### A. The stone and the artisan — §XIX

109. {Why does Benjamin return to _The Alexandrite_ when discussing inanimate nature?} : {The gemstone lets storytelling connect what appears lifeless and remote from human affairs with the historical destiny of a human world.}
    
110. {Why does Wenzel interpret the stone’s changing colors through the fate of Alexander II?} : {His reading turns material appearance into a symbolic relation between hope, bloodshed, and historical experience rather than treating the stone only as a classified mineral.}
    
111. {Why does the disagreement over whether the stone’s discoverer was a scholar or a magician matter?} : {It stages the tension between scientific identification and an imaginative understanding in which natural objects participate in human fate.}
    
112. {Why does Benjamin associate Wenzel’s craftsmanship with access to the inner meaning of created things?} : {Sustained attention to material gives the artisan an intimate relationship with objects through which qualities overlooked by detached classification can become perceptible.}
    

### B. Soul, eye, and hand — §XIX

113. {Why does Benjamin use Valéry’s account of artistic observation to connect soul, eye, and hand?} : {The connection presents making as an integrated activity in which inward responsiveness, perception, and practiced bodily skill cooperate.}
    
114. {Why does Benjamin insist that storytelling involves the hand as well as the voice?} : {Gestures shaped by practical work participate in narration, making the communication of experience a bodily performance.}
    
115. {How does the storyteller’s treatment of human life resemble an artisan’s treatment of material?} : {The storyteller shapes personal and received experience into a form that is distinctive, durable, and useful to others.}
    

### C. Proverbs, wisdom, and the whole life — §XIX

116. {Why does Benjamin compare a proverb to the surviving ruin of an old story?} : {The concise saying preserves a trace of narrated experience even after the fuller situation that gave it meaning has disappeared.}
    
117. {Why does Benjamin place the storyteller among teachers and sages?} : {The storyteller draws on a broad range of accumulated experience and can therefore offer guidance across many different circumstances.}
    
118. {Why does the storyteller’s life include experiences received from other people?} : {A narrated lifetime is enlarged by listening and transmission, so its resources exceed what one person has directly undergone.}
    
119. {What does Benjamin’s final image of a life being consumed by the gentle flame of its story suggest?} : {It presents narration as the sustained transformation of a mortal life into experience that can illuminate and accompany the lives of others.}
    
120. {Why does Benjamin end by connecting the storyteller with the righteous person?} : {The connection gives storytelling an ethical significance, presenting the shaping and sharing of experience as a practice of care through which a life becomes useful to others.}

## Topic 1: Chromatography Principles

### 1.1 Separation and retention

1. {What is the basic principle of chromatography?} : {Chromatography separates analytes because they distribute differently between the mobile and stationary phases and therefore travel through the system at different rates.}/{mobile phase}, {stationary phases}, {different rates}
    
2. {What information does a chromatogram provide?} : {A chromatogram plots detector response against time, with peak positions supporting identification and calibrated peak areas or heights supporting quantification.}/{detector response}, {peak positions}, {identification}, {quantification}
    
3. {How do retention time, hold-up time, and adjusted retention time differ?} : {Retention time tᵣ measures the time from injection to an analyte’s peak maximum, hold-up time tₘ measures the passage of an unretained substance, and adjusted retention time is tᵣ′ = tᵣ − tₘ.}/{retention time}, {hold-up time}, {unretained substance}, {tᵣ′ = tᵣ − tₘ}
    
4. {What is the retention factor, k?} : {The retention factor describes retention relative to an unretained substance and is calculated as k = (tᵣ − tₘ)/tₘ.}/{retention factor}, {unretained substance}, {k = (tᵣ − tₘ)/tₘ}
    
5. {How are the distribution coefficient and retention factor related?} : {The distribution coefficient is K = Cₛ/Cₘ for equilibrium concentrations in the stationary and mobile phases, while the retention factor is k = K(Vₛ/Vₘ), incorporating their relative volumes.}/{K = Cₛ/Cₘ}, {equilibrium concentrations}, {k = K(Vₛ/Vₘ)}, {relative volumes}
    
6. {What is chromatographic selectivity?} : {Chromatographic selectivity describes the relative retention of two analytes and is calculated as α = k₂/k₁, with k₂ > k₁.}/{chromatographic selectivity}, {relative retention}, {α = k₂/k₁}
    
7. {How does thin-layer chromatography work, and what is Rꜰ?} : {Thin-layer chromatography separates substances as solvent moves across a stationary layer, and Rꜰ is the distance travelled by an analyte spot divided by the distance travelled by the solvent front.}/{stationary layer}, {Rꜰ}, {analyte spot}, {solvent front}
    

### 1.2 Efficiency and peak broadening

8. {What do theoretical plates and plate height describe?} : {Theoretical plates represent hypothetical equilibration stages, and plate height H = L/N expresses column length per plate, with a smaller H indicating greater efficiency.}/{theoretical plates}, {equilibration stages}, {H = L/N}, {greater efficiency}
    
9. {How does increasing column length affect separation?} : {Increasing column length generally increases theoretical plate number and improves separation, but also increases analysis time and, in liquid chromatography, pressure requirements.}/{column length}, {theoretical plate number}, {analysis time}, {pressure requirements}
    
10. {What is the van Deemter equation?} : {The van Deemter equation is H = A + B/u + Cu, where u is mobile-phase linear velocity and the terms describe eddy dispersion, longitudinal diffusion, and resistance to mass transfer.}/{H = A + B/u + Cu}, {eddy dispersion}, {longitudinal diffusion}, {resistance to mass transfer}
    
11. {How do the three van Deemter terms cause peak broadening?} : {Eddy dispersion arises from different paths through packing, longitudinal diffusion spreads molecules along the column especially at low velocity, and resistance to mass transfer delays equilibration especially at high velocity.}/{different paths}, {longitudinal diffusion}, {low velocity}, {resistance to mass transfer}, {high velocity}
    
12. {Why is there an optimum mobile-phase velocity?} : {The optimum mobile-phase velocity minimises plate height by balancing longitudinal diffusion at low velocity against mass-transfer broadening at high velocity.}/{optimum mobile-phase velocity}, {plate height}, {longitudinal diffusion}, {mass-transfer broadening}
    

## Topic 2: Gas Chromatography

### 2.1 Applications and carrier gases

13. {Which substances are suitable for gas chromatography?} : {Gas chromatography analyses substances that can be vaporised without unacceptable decomposition, including many volatile pollutants, alcohols, industrial by-products, and suitable drug-related compounds.}/{vaporised}, {decomposition}, {volatile pollutants}, {alcohols}
    
14. {What are the main components of a gas chromatograph?} : {A gas chromatograph contains a carrier-gas supply, flow controller, injector, column inside a temperature-controlled oven, detector, and data system.}/{carrier-gas supply}, {injector}, {temperature-controlled oven}, {detector}
    
15. {What properties must a carrier gas have, and how is it purified?} : {A carrier gas must be chemically unreactive towards the analytes, dry, and sufficiently pure, with adsorbents, molecular sieves, and oxygen traps removing hydrocarbons, water, and oxygen.}/{chemically unreactive}, {dry}, {adsorbents}, {molecular sieves}, {oxygen traps}
    
16. {How do nitrogen, helium, and hydrogen compare as carrier gases?} : {Nitrogen has a relatively narrow efficiency optimum at lower velocity, whereas helium and especially hydrogen allow higher velocities with a smaller loss of efficiency.}/{nitrogen}, {helium}, {hydrogen}, {higher velocities}, {efficiency}
    

### 2.2 Injection methods

17. {What is the difference between split and splitless injection?} : {Split injection transfers a controlled fraction of the sample to prevent overload from concentrated samples, whereas splitless injection transfers most of the sample to improve sensitivity for low concentrations.}/{split injection}, {controlled fraction}, {overload}, {splitless injection}, {sensitivity}
    
18. {Why is an initially cool column useful during splitless injection?} : {An initially cool column focuses analytes near its entrance before heating, reducing initial band spreading and improving separation.}/{initially cool column}, {focuses analytes}, {band spreading}, {separation}
    
19. {What is programmed-temperature vaporisation, PTV?} : {PTV controls inlet temperature to permit solvent venting followed by analyte transfer, supporting trace analysis while reducing overload and discrimination between compounds of different volatility.}/{PTV}, {inlet temperature}, {solvent venting}, {trace analysis}, {discrimination}
    
20. {How do cold on-column injection and headspace sampling differ?} : {Cold on-column injection introduces liquid directly into a relatively cool column for heat-sensitive analytes, whereas headspace sampling introduces vapour above a sample to analyse sufficiently volatile components.}/{cold on-column injection}, {heat-sensitive analytes}, {headspace sampling}, {vapour}, {volatile components}
    
21. {How are gaseous samples injected reproducibly?} : {A valve introduces gaseous samples using a fixed-volume sample loop whose contents are transferred into the carrier-gas stream.}/{valve}, {fixed-volume sample loop}, {carrier-gas stream}
    

### 2.3 Columns, temperature, and detectors

24. {How do FID and TCD detect analytes?} : {FID measures ion current generated when suitable compounds burn in a hydrogen–air flame, whereas TCD measures changes in thermal conductivity relative to the carrier gas.}/{FID}, {ion current}, {hydrogen–air flame}, {TCD}, {thermal conductivity}

### 4.2 Vibrations and selection rules

## Topic 6: Mass Spectrometry

### 6.2 Mass analysers

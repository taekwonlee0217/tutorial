# Organized Organic Reactions

## Topic 1: Nucleophilic Substitution

### A. SN1 reactions

1. {What happens in an SN1 reaction?} : {The leaving group departs to form a carbocation, which is then attacked by a nucleophile.}
    
2. {What is the rate law for an SN1 reaction?} : {The rate is k[substrate] because carbocation formation is the rate-determining step.}
    
3. {Which substrates usually favor SN1 reactions?} : {Tertiary, benzylic, and allylic substrates favor SN1 reactions because they can form relatively stable carbocations.}
    
4. {Which solvents favor SN1 reactions?} : {Polar ionizing solvents, often protic solvents such as water or alcohols, favor SN1 reactions by stabilizing developing ions.}
    
5. {What stereochemical outcome is expected from SN1 substitution at a chiral carbon?} : {Attack on either face of the planar carbocation produces racemization, often with unequal amounts of retention and inversion.}
    
6. {Why can rearrangements occur during SN1 reactions?} : {The carbocation intermediate can undergo hydride or alkyl shifts to form a more stable carbocation.}
    

### B. SN2 reactions

7. {What happens in an SN2 reaction?} : {A nucleophile attacks from the backside while the leaving group departs in one concerted step.}
    
8. {What is the rate law for an SN2 reaction?} : {The rate is k[substrate][nucleophile] because both reactants participate in the rate-determining step.}
    
9. {How does substrate substitution affect SN2 reactivity?} : {SN2 reactivity generally decreases in the order methyl > primary > secondary, while tertiary centers resist SN2 substitution.}
    
10. {Which solvents commonly favor SN2 reactions with anionic nucleophiles?} : {Polar aprotic solvents such as DMSO and DMF favor SN2 reactions by avoiding strong hydrogen-bond solvation of the nucleophile.}
    
11. {What stereochemical change occurs in an SN2 reaction?} : {Backside attack causes inversion of configuration at the reacting carbon.}
    
12. {Does SN2 inversion always change an R designation into S?} : {No, the R or S designation also depends on how substituent priorities change after substitution.}
    
13. {Why are carbocation rearrangements absent from SN2 reactions?} : {SN2 reactions proceed without a carbocation intermediate.}
    

### C. Leaving groups and common substitutions

14. {What makes a good leaving group?} : {A good leaving group stabilizes the electron pair it receives and is usually a weak base.}
    
15. {What is the usual leaving-group order among halides in alkyl substitution?} : {The usual order is I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ ≫ F⁻.}
    
16. {How can an alcohol be converted into a better substrate for substitution?} : {Its hydroxyl group can be protonated or converted into a sulfonate ester such as a tosylate or mesylate.}
    
17. {What happens when an alkyl halide undergoes substitution with hydroxide?} : {The halide is replaced by OH to form an alcohol.}
    
18. {What happens when a suitable alkyl halide reacts with cyanide?} : {Carbon attack by cyanide produces a nitrile and adds one carbon to the carbon skeleton.}
    
19. {What happens when an alkyl halide reacts with a thiolate?} : {Substitution replaces the halide with SR to form a thioether.}
    
20. {What is the Williamson ether synthesis?} : {An alkoxide reacts with a suitable methyl or primary alkyl electrophile by SN2 substitution to form an ether.}
    
21. {Why are tertiary alkyl halides unsuitable for the usual Williamson ether synthesis?} : {An alkoxide generally promotes elimination rather than SN2 substitution at a tertiary carbon.}
    
22. {What happens when an alcohol reacts with TsCl and a base?} : {It forms a tosylate while retaining the configuration of the carbon bearing the oxygen.}
    
23. {What happens in a Mitsunobu reaction?} : {An alcohol reacts with a suitable acidic nucleophile using PPh₃ and an azodicarboxylate reagent, usually with inversion at the alcohol-bearing carbon.}
    
24. {How does the Gabriel synthesis prepare a primary amine?} : {Phthalimide anion alkylates a suitable alkyl halide, and subsequent cleavage releases the primary amine.}
    

## Topic 2: Elimination Reactions

### A. E1, E2, and E1cB mechanisms

25. {What happens in an E1 reaction?} : {The leaving group departs to form a carbocation, followed by removal of a β-hydrogen to form an alkene.}
    
26. {What is the rate law for a typical E1 reaction?} : {The rate is k[substrate] because carbocation formation is rate-determining.}
    
27. {What happens in an E2 reaction?} : {A base removes a β-hydrogen while the leaving group departs and the double bond forms in one concerted step.}
    
28. {What is the rate law for an E2 reaction?} : {The rate is k[substrate][base].}
    
29. {What happens in an E1cB reaction?} : {Deprotonation first forms a stabilized conjugate base, which then eliminates a leaving group to form a double bond.}
    
30. {When is E1cB elimination particularly important?} : {It is important when the substrate contains an acidic hydrogen and a poor leaving group, as in many base-promoted aldol dehydrations.}
    
31. {Which conditions often favor elimination over substitution?} : {Strong bulky bases and higher temperatures often favor elimination over substitution.}
    
32. {Which competing mechanisms can produce rearranged products?} : {SN1 and E1 can produce rearranged products because both involve carbocation intermediates.}
    

### B. Regioselectivity and stereochemistry

33. {What does Zaitsev’s rule predict?} : {Many eliminations favor the more substituted alkene when competing structural and stereochemical factors permit it.}
    
34. {What is the Hofmann product?} : {The Hofmann product is the less substituted alkene, often favored by bulky bases or elimination from quaternary ammonium salts.}
    
35. {What geometry is usually required for E2 elimination?} : {The β-C–H bond and C–leaving-group bond must be anti-periplanar.}
    
36. {What geometry is required for ordinary E2 elimination in a cyclohexane chair?} : {The leaving group and the eliminated β-hydrogen must occupy trans-diaxial positions.}
    
37. {Why can E2 elimination be stereospecific?} : {The required anti-periplanar arrangement links the substrate configuration to the geometry of the alkene product.}
    
38. {Why can small bridged bicyclic compounds resist bridgehead alkene formation?} : {Their ring geometry prevents the bridgehead carbon from adopting the geometry required for a stable conventional double bond.}
    

### C. Useful elimination reactions

39. {How are alcohols dehydrated to alkenes?} : {Heating with an acid such as H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄ promotes loss of water and formation of an alkene.}
    
40. {How can a vicinal or geminal dihalide be converted into an alkyne?} : {Two successive dehydrohalogenations with a sufficiently strong base, commonly NaNH₂, form the triple bond.}
    
41. {What is a Cope elimination?} : {Heating an amine oxide produces an alkene by an intramolecular syn elimination.}
    
42. {What is the Chugaev elimination?} : {Heating an alcohol-derived xanthate produces an alkene by a syn elimination.}
    
43. {What does the Burgess reagent do?} : {It dehydrates suitable alcohols to alkenes through a syn elimination pathway.}
    

## Topic 3: Addition to Alkenes and Alkynes

### A. Hydrohalogenation and hydration

44. {What happens when an alkene reacts with HX under ordinary ionic conditions?} : {Hydrogen and halide add across the double bond, usually with Markovnikov regioselectivity.}
    
45. {What does Markovnikov regioselectivity mean for HX addition?} : {Hydrogen generally adds to the less substituted carbon and halide to the more substituted carbon.}
    
46. {Why can ordinary acid-catalyzed alkene additions produce rearrangements?} : {Their carbocation intermediates can undergo hydride or alkyl shifts before nucleophilic attack.}
    
47. {What happens during acid-catalyzed hydration of an alkene?} : {Water adds across the double bond to form an alcohol, usually with Markovnikov regioselectivity.}
    
48. {What happens during oxymercuration–demercuration?} : {An alkene is converted into a Markovnikov alcohol without the usual carbocation rearrangements.}
    
49. {What happens during hydroboration–oxidation of an alkene?} : {BH₃ followed by H₂O₂ and base forms an anti-Markovnikov alcohol through overall syn addition.}
    
50. {What does syn addition mean?} : {The two newly introduced groups add to the same face of the π bond.}
    
51. {What does anti addition mean?} : {The two newly introduced groups add to opposite faces of the π bond.}
    
52. {What happens during mercury-catalyzed hydration of a terminal alkyne?} : {An enol forms and usually tautomerizes to a methyl ketone.}
    
53. {What happens during hydroboration–oxidation of a terminal alkyne using a suitable bulky borane?} : {An enol forms and tautomerizes to an aldehyde.}
    

### B. Halogenation and oxygen-containing products

54. {What happens when an alkene reacts with Br₂ in a non-nucleophilic solvent?} : {Anti addition through a bromonium intermediate produces a vicinal dibromide.}
    
55. {What happens when an alkene reacts with Br₂ in water?} : {A bromohydrin forms with anti addition and OH usually attached to the more substituted carbon.}
    
56. {What happens when an alkene reacts with Br₂ in methanol?} : {A bromoether forms with anti addition and OCH₃ usually attached to the more substituted carbon.}
    
57. {What happens when an alkene reacts with mCPBA?} : {A stereospecific oxygen transfer converts the alkene into an epoxide.}
    
58. {How does alkene geometry affect epoxide geometry?} : {A Z alkene gives the corresponding cis epoxide and an E alkene gives the corresponding trans epoxide.}
    
59. {What happens when an alkene reacts with OsO₄ followed by workup?} : {Syn dihydroxylation produces a vicinal diol.}
    
60. {How can an alkene be converted into an anti vicinal diol?} : {Epoxidation followed by aqueous acid-catalyzed ring opening produces an anti diol.}
    
61. {What happens when a vicinal diol reacts with NaIO₄?} : {The bond between the hydroxyl-bearing carbons is cleaved to form carbonyl compounds.}
    

### C. Cleavage, reduction, and cyclopropanation

62. {What happens during ozonolysis with reductive workup?} : {The alkene double bond is cleaved to produce aldehydes and/or ketones.}
    
63. {What happens during ozonolysis with oxidative workup?} : {Double-bond cleavage gives ketones and oxidizes initially formed aldehydes to carboxylic acids.}
    
64. {What happens when an alkene reacts with H₂ and Pd, Pt, or Ni?} : {Catalytic hydrogenation converts the double bond into a single bond by addition of hydrogen.}
    
65. {What happens when an alkyne reacts with H₂ and Lindlar catalyst?} : {Partial syn hydrogenation produces a Z alkene when E/Z geometry is possible.}
    
66. {What happens when an alkyne reacts with sodium in liquid ammonia?} : {Dissolving-metal reduction produces an E alkene when E/Z geometry is possible.}
    
67. {What happens in the Simmons–Smith reaction?} : {CH₂I₂ and a zinc reagent convert an alkene into a cyclopropane while preserving its relative substituent stereochemistry.}
    

## Topic 4: Carbonyl Addition and Protecting Groups

### A. Carbonyl reactivity

68. {Why is a carbonyl carbon electrophilic?} : {Polarization of the C=O bond leaves the carbon partially positive and susceptible to nucleophilic attack.}
    
69. {Why are aldehydes generally more reactive than ketones toward nucleophilic addition?} : {Aldehydes usually have less steric hindrance and less electron donation from alkyl substituents.}
    
70. {What happens when water adds to an aldehyde or ketone?} : {A reversible nucleophilic addition forms a geminal diol, also called a carbonyl hydrate.}
    
71. {What happens when HCN adds to an aldehyde or ketone?} : {A cyanohydrin forms with OH and CN attached to the former carbonyl carbon.}
    

### B. Acetals, imines, and enamines

72. {What happens when an aldehyde or ketone reacts with excess alcohol under acid catalysis?} : {An acetal forms when the equilibrium is driven by removing water.}
    
73. {How can a carbonyl group be protected using ethylene glycol?} : {Acid-catalyzed reaction with ethylene glycol forms a cyclic acetal.}
    
74. {How is an acetal protecting group removed?} : {Aqueous acid hydrolyzes the acetal to regenerate the carbonyl compound.}
    
75. {Why can acetals protect carbonyl groups during Grignard reactions?} : {Acetals generally tolerate the basic nucleophilic conditions that would otherwise cause addition to the carbonyl group.}
    
76. {What happens when an aldehyde or ketone reacts with a primary amine?} : {Condensation with loss of water forms an imine under suitable conditions.}
    
77. {What happens when an enolizable aldehyde or ketone reacts with a secondary amine?} : {Condensation forms an enamine under suitable conditions.}
    
78. {What is reductive amination?} : {A carbonyl compound reacts with an amine to form an imine or iminium species that is reduced to an amine.}
    
79. {Why is NaBH₃CN useful in reductive amination?} : {Under suitable mildly acidic conditions it preferentially reduces iminium species.}
    

### C. Carbonyl olefination and epoxide formation

80. {What happens in a Wittig reaction?} : {A phosphorus ylide converts an aldehyde or ketone into an alkene while forming triphenylphosphine oxide.}
    
81. {How is a Wittig reagent prepared from a suitable alkyl halide?} : {Reaction with PPh₃ forms a phosphonium salt that is deprotonated to generate the ylide.}
    
82. {What happens in a Darzens reaction?} : {A base-generated α-haloester enolate reacts with a carbonyl compound and cyclizes to form an epoxy ester.}
    
83. {What happens when a suitable sulfur ylide reacts with an aldehyde or ketone?} : {Carbonyl addition followed by ring closure produces an epoxide.}
    

## Topic 5: Organometallic Reactions

### A. Formation and compatibility

84. {How is a Grignard reagent prepared?} : {An organic halide reacts with magnesium in dry ether or THF to form RMgX.}
    
85. {Why must Grignard reactions be performed under dry conditions?} : {Water protonates the reagent and destroys its carbon nucleophile.}
    
86. {Which functional groups commonly interfere with Grignard reagents through acid–base reactions?} : {Unprotected OH, NH, SH, and COOH groups can protonate and consume the reagent.}
    
87. {What happens when RMgX reacts with water?} : {Protonation converts the organic group into the hydrocarbon RH.}
    
88. {What happens when RMgX reacts with D₂O?} : {Deuteration produces RD with deuterium at the carbon previously bonded to magnesium.}
    
89. {What happens when a Grignard reagent reacts with a terminal alkyne?} : {It can remove the acidic terminal hydrogen to form a magnesium acetylide and the corresponding hydrocarbon.}
    

### B. Carbon–carbon bond formation

90. {What happens when a Grignard reagent reacts with formaldehyde followed by acidic workup?} : {A primary alcohol containing one additional carbon is formed.}
    
91. {What happens when a Grignard reagent reacts with another aldehyde followed by acidic workup?} : {A secondary alcohol is formed.}
    
92. {What happens when a Grignard reagent reacts with a ketone followed by acidic workup?} : {A tertiary alcohol is formed.}
    
93. {What happens when an ordinary ester reacts with excess Grignard reagent followed by workup?} : {Two additions generally produce a tertiary alcohol containing two groups from the Grignard reagent.}
    
94. {What happens when a Grignard reagent reacts with CO₂ followed by acidic workup?} : {A carboxylic acid with one additional carbon is formed.}
    
95. {What happens when a Grignard reagent reacts with ethylene oxide followed by workup?} : {A primary alcohol is formed with a two-carbon extension of the original organic group.}
    
96. {What happens when a Grignard reagent reacts with a nitrile followed by acidic hydrolysis?} : {Addition forms an imine-derived intermediate that hydrolyzes to a ketone.}
    
97. {What happens when an acetylide reacts with a suitable primary alkyl halide?} : {SN2 alkylation forms a new carbon–carbon bond and extends the alkyne chain.}
    
98. {What is the usual difference between Grignard and organocuprate addition to an enone?} : {Grignard reagents commonly favor 1,2-addition, whereas organocuprates commonly favor 1,4-addition.}
    

## Topic 6: Oxidation and Reduction

### A. Alcohol oxidation

99. {What happens when a primary alcohol is oxidized with PCC under anhydrous conditions?} : {It is generally converted into an aldehyde.}
    
100. {What happens when a primary alcohol undergoes Swern oxidation?} : {Activated DMSO followed by base converts it into an aldehyde.}
    
101. {What happens when a primary alcohol is oxidized with aqueous Jones reagent?} : {It is generally converted into a carboxylic acid.}
    
102. {What happens when a secondary alcohol is oxidized with PCC, Swern reagent, or Jones reagent?} : {It is converted into a ketone.}
    
103. {Why do ordinary alcohol oxidants usually fail to oxidize tertiary alcohols cleanly?} : {Tertiary alcohols lack a hydrogen on the hydroxyl-bearing carbon, so ordinary carbonyl formation cannot occur without carbon–carbon bond cleavage.}
    

### B. Hydride reductions

104. {What does NaBH₄ commonly reduce?} : {It commonly reduces aldehydes and ketones to primary and secondary alcohols, respectively.}
    
105. {What does LiAlH₄ do to ordinary esters?} : {It reduces the acyl portion to a primary alcohol and releases the alkoxy portion as an alcohol after workup.}
    
106. {What does LiAlH₄ do to carboxylic acids?} : {It reduces them to primary alcohols after workup.}
    
107. {What does LiAlH₄ do to amides?} : {It reduces them to amines by replacing the carbonyl oxygen with hydrogen atoms.}
    
108. {What does LiAlH₄ do to nitriles?} : {It reduces them to primary amines after workup.}
    
109. {What can carefully controlled DIBAL-H reduction do to an ester?} : {At low temperature with controlled reagent amounts it can give an aldehyde after workup.}
    
110. {Why is an unspecified DIBAL-H reaction insufficient to predict selective reduction of a free carboxylic acid to an aldehyde?} : {The outcome depends on substrate and conditions, so a suitable derivative and a validated selective reduction procedure must be specified.}
    

## Topic 7: Enolates and Carbon–Carbon Bond Formation

### A. Enolate formation and alkylation

111. {What is an enolate?} : {An enolate is a resonance-stabilized anion formed by removing an α-hydrogen from a carbonyl compound.}
    
112. {Why are α-hydrogens of carbonyl compounds acidic?} : {Their removal produces an anion stabilized by resonance with the carbonyl group.}
    
113. {What does LDA commonly do to an enolizable carbonyl compound?} : {It removes an α-hydrogen to form an enolate while minimizing direct nucleophilic addition.}
    
114. {What is a kinetic enolate?} : {It is the enolate formed fastest, often by deprotonation at the less hindered α-position.}
    
115. {What is a thermodynamic enolate?} : {It is the more stable enolate favored when reversible conditions allow equilibration.}
    
116. {What happens when an enolate reacts with a suitable primary alkyl halide?} : {Carbon alkylation usually forms a new carbon–carbon bond at the α-position.}
    
117. {What happens in a Stork enamine reaction?} : {An enamine undergoes alkylation or acylation and is then hydrolyzed to regenerate the carbonyl group.}
    

### B. Aldol reactions

118. {What happens in an aldol addition?} : {An enolate or enol attacks another carbonyl compound to form a β-hydroxy aldehyde or ketone.}
    
119. {What happens in an aldol condensation?} : {An aldol addition product loses water to form an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound.}
    
120. {What structural requirement allows a compound to act as an ordinary aldol enolate donor?} : {It must contain at least one removable α-hydrogen.}
    
121. {How can a crossed aldol reaction be made more selective?} : {A preformed enolate or a non-enolizable carbonyl acceptor can reduce competing product formation.}
    
122. {Which ring sizes are commonly favored in intramolecular aldol reactions?} : {Five- and six-membered rings are commonly favored when the substrate permits their formation.}
    

### C. Claisen and Dieckmann condensations

123. {What happens in a Claisen condensation?} : {An ester enolate acylates another ester to form a β-keto ester after workup.}
    
124. {Why is an alkoxide matching the ester’s alkoxy group commonly used in a Claisen condensation?} : {It limits unwanted ester exchange by transesterification.}
    
125. {What drives a conventional Claisen condensation toward its product?} : {Deprotonation of the acidic β-keto ester product helps drive the equilibrium forward.}
    
126. {What is a Dieckmann condensation?} : {It is an intramolecular Claisen condensation that converts a suitable diester into a cyclic β-keto ester.}
    
127. {What happens when a β-keto acid is heated?} : {It loses CO₂ and forms a carbonyl compound after enol–keto tautomerization.}
    

### D. Michael addition, annulation, and umpolung

128. {What happens in a Michael addition?} : {A suitable nucleophile adds to the β-carbon of an α,β-unsaturated electron-withdrawing system.}
    
129. {What is the usual carbonyl outcome of 1,2-addition to an enone?} : {Attack at the carbonyl carbon gives an allylic alcohol after workup.}
    
130. {What is the usual carbonyl outcome of 1,4-addition to an enone?} : {Attack at the β-carbon gives a saturated carbonyl compound after protonation and tautomerization.}
    
131. {What is a Robinson annulation?} : {A Michael addition followed by intramolecular aldol condensation constructs a six-membered enone ring.}
    
132. {What does umpolung mean in carbonyl chemistry?} : {It means reversing the usual electrophilic character of a carbonyl carbon so that it behaves as a nucleophile.}
    
133. {What happens in the Corey–Seebach reaction?} : {A deprotonated dithiane acts as an acyl-anion equivalent and reacts with an electrophile before carbonyl regeneration.}
    
134. {What happens in a benzoin condensation?} : {Cyanide or an N-heterocyclic carbene catalyzes coupling of suitable aldehydes to form an α-hydroxy ketone.}
    
135. {What happens in a Stetter reaction?} : {An N-heterocyclic carbene generates an aldehyde-derived acyl-anion equivalent that adds to a Michael acceptor.}
    

## Topic 8: Carboxylic Acid Derivatives

### A. Acyl substitution

136. {What happens in nucleophilic acyl substitution?} : {A nucleophile adds to an acyl carbon and the resulting tetrahedral intermediate eliminates a leaving group.}
    
137. {What is the usual reactivity order of common carboxylic acid derivatives?} : {Acyl chlorides generally react faster than anhydrides, esters, and amides.}
    
138. {What happens when a carboxylic acid reacts with SOCl₂?} : {It forms an acyl chloride with release of SO₂ and HCl.}
    
139. {What happens when an acyl chloride reacts with an alcohol?} : {Nucleophilic acyl substitution forms an ester.}
    
140. {What happens when an acyl chloride reacts with ammonia or a primary or secondary amine?} : {Nucleophilic acyl substitution forms an amide.}
    
141. {What happens in Fischer esterification?} : {A carboxylic acid and an alcohol reversibly form an ester and water under acid catalysis.}
    
142. {What happens during ester saponification?} : {Base-promoted hydrolysis produces a carboxylate salt and an alcohol.}
    
143. {What happens when a lactone undergoes hydrolysis?} : {Its cyclic ester bond opens to form a hydroxy acid or hydroxy carboxylate.}
    

### B. Diagnostic and related reactions

144. {Which compounds give the haloform reaction?} : {Methyl ketones and compounds oxidizable to methyl ketones can give haloform under suitable halogen–base conditions.}
    
145. {What happens when a methyl ketone reacts with I₂ and NaOH?} : {It is cleaved into a carboxylate salt and yellow iodoform, CHI₃.}
    
146. {What happens when an aldehyde without α-hydrogens undergoes a Cannizzaro reaction?} : {Strong base causes disproportionation to an alcohol and a carboxylate.}
    

## Topic 9: Aromatic and Pericyclic Reactions

### A. Electrophilic aromatic substitution

147. {What happens when benzene reacts with Br₂ and iron or FeBr₃?} : {Electrophilic aromatic substitution produces bromobenzene and HBr.}
    
148. {Why does benzene favor bromination by substitution over ordinary bromine addition?} : {Substitution restores aromaticity, whereas addition would leave a nonaromatic product.}
    
149. {What are the main steps of electrophilic aromatic substitution?} : {The aromatic ring attacks an electrophile to form a σ-complex and then loses a proton to restore aromaticity.}
    
150. {What happens when benzene reacts with concentrated HNO₃ and H₂SO₄?} : {Nitration forms nitrobenzene through attack on the nitronium ion.}
    
151. {What happens in a Friedel–Crafts acylation?} : {An aromatic ring reacts with an activated acyl reagent to form an aryl ketone.}
    
152. {Why is Friedel–Crafts acylation useful for installing a straight-chain alkyl substituent?} : {Acylation avoids alkyl-carbocation rearrangements and subsequent carbonyl reduction gives the corresponding alkyl group.}
    
153. {Which common substituents direct aromatic substitution mainly to ortho and para positions?} : {Electron-donating groups such as OH, OR, NH₂, and alkyl groups usually direct ortho and para.}
    
154. {Which common substituents direct aromatic substitution mainly to meta positions?} : {Strong electron-withdrawing groups such as NO₂, CN, CHO, COR, and COOH usually direct meta.}
    
155. {How do halogens affect electrophilic aromatic substitution?} : {Halogens deactivate the ring but direct substitution mainly to ortho and para positions.}
    
156. {What happens when aniline reacts with NaNO₂ and acid at low temperature?} : {It is converted into a benzenediazonium salt.}
    
157. {What happens when an aryl diazonium salt reacts under suitable Sandmeyer conditions?} : {The diazonium group is replaced by groups such as Cl, Br, or CN with release of nitrogen.}
    

### B. Diels–Alder reactions

158. {What happens in a Diels–Alder reaction?} : {A conjugated diene and a dienophile undergo a concerted [4+2] cycloaddition to form a six-membered ring.}
    
159. {What conformation must the diene adopt for a Diels–Alder reaction?} : {The diene must adopt an s-cis conformation.}
    
160. {How is dienophile stereochemistry transferred in a Diels–Alder reaction?} : {The relative cis or trans arrangement of dienophile substituents is preserved in the product.}
    
161. {Is the endo product always favored in a Diels–Alder reaction?} : {No, substrate geometry, steric effects, and reaction conditions can favor an exo product.}

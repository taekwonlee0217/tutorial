# Catalysis Question Catalogue

## 1. Core Concepts, Metrics, and Kinetics

### Definitions and catalyst performance

1. {Define atom economy and give two particularly atom-economic reactions.} : {Atom economy is the fraction of reactant atoms incorporated into the desired product, and examples with essentially 100% atom economy are hydrogenation, RCH=CHR' + H2 -> RCH2-CH2R', and a Diels-Alder cycloaddition, diene + dienophile -> cyclohexene adduct.}

2. {Explain catalysis with an energy-versus-reaction-coordinate diagram compared with an uncatalysed reaction.} : {A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation free energy, shown by a lower transition-state peak on the energy profile, while leaving the reactant-product free-energy difference and equilibrium unchanged.}

3. {Explain TON and TOF for A -> B when 100 mmol A gives 89% B with 0.1 mol% catalyst and reaches 70% conversion after 60 minutes.} : {TON is moles of product per mole of catalyst and equals 89 mmol/0.10 mmol = 890 at 89% yield, whereas the average TOF after 60 minutes is 70 mmol/(0.10 mmol x 1 h) = 700 h<sup>-1</sup>.}

4. {Use the four turnover-time curves to compare catalyst productivity and activity.} : {Curve 4 has the greatest initial activity but unknown lifetime, curve 1 has moderate activity and the highest observed plateau productivity, curve 2 starts more rapidly but deactivates at a lower total productivity, and curve 3 has low constant activity yet can achieve the greatest long-term productivity because it remains active.}

5. {What is a volcano curve, for example for the Haber-Bosch process, and what can be concluded from it?} : {A volcano curve plots catalytic activity against adsorption energy and shows that maximum activity occurs at intermediate binding strength because weak binding prevents activation while strong binding blocks turnover and desorption.}

6. {Which statement correctly expresses the Sabatier principle, and why?} : {Statement (a) is correct because catalyst-substrate binding must be strong enough for adsorption and activation but weak enough for subsequent reaction and product desorption.}

7. {What does the volcano curve describe in heterogeneous catalysis, and give an example with justification.} : {Answer (b) is correct because a volcano curve relates metal activity to reactant or intermediate binding strength, as in ammonia synthesis where weak-binding metals scarcely dissociate N2 and strong-binding metals retain surface nitrogen, while an intermediate binder such as promoted iron balances activation and turnover.}

### Reaction kinetics and energy

43. {State the Arrhenius equation, express how the styrene-polymerisation rate-constant ratio changes from 50 to 75 degrees C, and explain how to determine the activation energy.} : {The Arrhenius equation is k = A exp(-E<sub>a</sub>/RT), so k<sub>75</sub>/k<sub>50</sub> = exp[(E<sub>a</sub>/R)(1/323.15 - 1/348.15)] and E<sub>a</sub> is obtained from the slope -E<sub>a</sub>/R of a plot of ln k versus 1/T measured at several temperatures.}

44. {What is transition-state theory?} : {Transition-state theory treats reactants and an activated complex as a quasi-equilibrium separated by a free-energy barrier and gives k = (k<sub>B</sub>T/h)exp(-DeltaG<sup>double dagger</sup>/RT), represented by the maximum on a free-energy reaction-coordinate profile.}

45. {What is a pseudo-first-order reaction?} : {A pseudo-first-order reaction is intrinsically of higher order but follows v = k<sub>obs</sub>[A] because another reactant is present in such large excess that its concentration is effectively constant.}

46. {What is autocatalysis, and give an example.} : {Autocatalysis occurs when a reaction product catalyses its own formation, as represented by A + B -> 2B and exemplified by acid-producing ester hydrolysis in which the generated H+ accelerates further hydrolysis, producing a sigmoidal concentration-time curve.}

47. {What does the Bronsted-Evans-Polanyi relation state?} : {The Bronsted-Evans-Polanyi relation states that activation energy varies approximately linearly with reaction enthalpy for a related reaction family, E<sub>a</sub> = E<sub>0</sub> + alpha DeltaH, so a plot of E<sub>a</sub> against DeltaH has slope alpha between zero and one.}

## 2. Industrial Catalytic Processes

### Ammonia and nitric acid

2. {Describe the Haber-Bosch process, ammonia separation, and the essential catalyst components.} : {Haber-Bosch compresses purified N2 and H2 in a 1:3 ratio, reacts them over promoted iron near 400-500 degrees C and 150-300 bar, condenses ammonia because it liquefies much more readily than N2 or H2, and recycles unreacted gas, using Fe derived from magnetite with promoters such as K2O, Al2O3, and CaO.}

3. {What are the elementary Haber-Bosch steps, and how is the nitrogen triple bond activated on the metal surface?} : {N2 and H2 adsorb, H2 dissociates, N2 binds end-on or side-on and accepts electron density by metal-to-pi-star back-donation before dissociating at suitable iron ensembles, surface N is hydrogenated stepwise through NH and NH2 to NH3, and ammonia desorbs.}

4. {What happens at higher or lower Haber-Bosch temperatures, and why are the current conditions used?} : {Higher temperature accelerates kinetics but lowers the exothermic reaction's equilibrium ammonia yield, lower temperature raises equilibrium yield but makes N2 activation too slow, and the industrial compromise combines moderate-high temperature, high pressure, catalyst activity, separation, and recycle.}

5. {Explain the Ostwald process, its catalyst, and SCR after-treatment.} : {In the Ostwald process NH3 is oxidised over Pt-Rh gauze to NO, NO is oxidised to NO2, and NO2 is absorbed in water to form HNO3, while selective catalytic reduction removes residual NOx by reacting it with NH3 or urea over catalysts such as V2O5-WO3/TiO2 to give N2 and H2O.}

### Large-scale carbonylation, oligomerisation, and polymerisation

25. {Explain the Rhone-Poulenc process for [^1]hydroformylation of an alkene such as propene.} : {The Rhone-Poulenc process uses a water-soluble Rh-TPPTS complex in an aqueous phase to hydroformylate propene with CO/H2 mainly to n-butanal, after which the organic product phase separates and the rhodium catalyst remains in water for recycle.}

26. {Describe classical Ziegler-Natta catalysis with titanium compounds and aluminium alkyls.} : {AlR3 alkylates and reduces TiCl4 on MgCl2 to an active Ti-alkyl site, olefin coordinates and inserts repeatedly into the Ti-C bond by the Cossee-Arlman mechanism, and chain transfer terminates growth while the supported chiral environment controls stereochemistry.}

27. {Sketch the SHOP cycle, explain generation of the active species, and name the product distribution.} : {In SHOP, a Ni(II) P,O-chelate precursor is converted to a Ni-H species, ethylene coordination and migratory insertion repeatedly extend a Ni-alkyl chain, beta-hydride elimination releases linear alpha-olefins and regenerates Ni-H, and the chain lengths follow a Schulz-Flory distribution.}

28. {Sketch the Monsanto acetic-acid cycle.} : {The Monsanto cycle proceeds by oxidative addition of CH3I to [RhI2(CO)2]<sup>-</sup>, CO coordination and methyl migration to a Rh-acyl, reductive elimination of CH3COI, hydrolysis to CH3CO2H and HI, and methanol carbonylation through regeneration of CH3I.}

## 3. Homogeneous Transition-Metal Catalysis

### Elementary organometallic steps and ligands

9. {Name four general reaction possibilities in homogeneous complex catalysis using a general metal complex.} : {Four central possibilities are oxidative addition at the metal, reductive elimination from it, migratory insertion of a coordinated ligand into a metal-ligand bond, and beta-hydride elimination from a metal alkyl, often accompanied by ligand association or dissociation.}

10. {Define the Tolman cone angle and position PF3, PH3, PPh3, and P(t-Bu)3 qualitatively by donor strength and steric demand.} : {The Tolman cone angle measures the ligand cone subtended at a metal 2.28 A from phosphorus, placing PH3 near 87 degrees with minimal steric demand, PF3 near 104 degrees as a weak sigma donor and strong pi acceptor, PPh3 near 145 degrees with intermediate donation and bulk, and P(t-Bu)3 near 182 degrees as a very strong, very bulky donor.}

11. {Why can Pd[P(t-Bu)3]2 couple 4-chlorotoluene when Pd(PPh3)4 cannot, and why is the two-coordinate complex air-stable?} : {P(t-Bu)3 is much more electron-donating and bulky than PPh3, so its coordinatively unsaturated Pd(0) centre undergoes oxidative addition of the strong aryl-Cl bond readily, while the bulky ligands sterically shield the metal sufficiently to give unexpected air stability.}

12. {What catalyst and ligand properties enable mild activation and coupling of aryl-Cl bonds?} : {Mild aryl-Cl activation requires an electron-rich, low-coordinate palladium centre supported by strongly donating, bulky ligands such as trialkylphosphanes or N-heterocyclic carbenes that accelerate oxidative addition while leaving an open site and supporting the later coupling steps.}

### Cross-coupling and carbonylation

11. {Give the general Pd(PPh3)4 cross-coupling cycle for Ar-X in Stille, Suzuki, or Negishi coupling.} : {Pd(0)L2 undergoes oxidative addition with Ar-X to form L2Pd(II)(Ar)X, transmetallation with R-SnR3, R-B(OH)2/base, or R-ZnX gives L2Pd(II)(Ar)R, and reductive elimination releases Ar-R and regenerates Pd(0)L2.}

12. {Name at least four cross-couplings, their transmetallating nucleophiles, and required additives.} : {Examples are Stille with R-SnBu3, Negishi with R-ZnX, Suzuki with R-B(OH)2 plus base to form a more nucleophilic boronate, and Hiyama with R-SiR3 plus F<sup>-</sup> or base to generate a hypervalent silicate, while Kumada with R-MgX is a fifth option needing no activating additive.}

13. {How are Pd(PPh3)4, dppf, and Josiphos prepared?} : {Pd(PPh3)4 is obtained by reducing a Pd(II) salt in excess PPh3, dppf by dilithiating ferrocene and trapping it with ClPPh2, and Josiphos by stereospecific phosphine substitution on a chiral 1-(dimethylamino)ethylferrocene followed by directed ortho lithiation and trapping with a second chlorophosphine.}

14. {Give the Pd-catalysed carbonylative cyclisation mechanism to the cyclic lactam, including precatalyst activation, elementary steps, and Pd oxidation states.} : {PdCl2(PPh3)2 must first be reduced from Pd(II) to Pd(0), after which Pd(0) oxidatively adds the aryl-I bond to Pd(II), CO coordinates and inserts into the Pd-aryl bond to give an acyl-Pd(II) species, intramolecular amine capture with Et3N-assisted deprotonation closes the lactam, and product-forming reductive elimination regenerates Pd(0).}

### Hydrogenation, hydroformylation, and hydrosilylation

16. {Give the Wilkinson-catalyst cycle for olefin hydrogenation and name the elementary steps.} : {RhCl(PPh3)3 loses PPh3, oxidatively adds H2 to Rh(I) to form a Rh(III) dihydride, binds the olefin, inserts it into Rh-H to form a Rh-alkyl hydride, reductively eliminates the alkane, and reassociates phosphane.}

17. {Explain a Curtin-Hammett diagram for asymmetric hydrogenation of a prochiral olefin with a chiral Rh complex.} : {Rapidly interconverting diastereomeric catalyst-substrate complexes lead through transition states of unequal free energy, so the pathway with the lower DeltaG<sup>double dagger</sup> forms the major enantiomer even if its precursor complex is less populated.}

18. {How is Wilkinson's catalyst prepared?} : {RhCl(PPh3)3 is prepared by refluxing RhCl3 hydrate with excess triphenylphosphine in ethanol, where phosphine reduces Rh(III) to Rh(I) and the burgundy complex crystallises.}

19. {Sketch the cobalt-carbonyl-catalysed hydroformylation mechanism.} : {HCo(CO)4 loses CO, coordinates the alkene, inserts it into Co-H to give a linear or branched cobalt alkyl, inserts CO to form an acyl, oxidatively adds H2, and releases the aldehyde while regenerating HCo(CO)4.}

20. {What is the modified Chalk-Harrod mechanism?} : {In the modified Chalk-Harrod hydrosilylation cycle the alkene coordinates to a metal hydride-silyl species, inserts into the metal-Si bond rather than the metal-H bond, and C-H reductive elimination releases the hydrosilane addition product and regenerates the catalyst.}

### Metathesis, cycloaddition, and polymerisation

18. {Explain olefin metathesis and name two catalyst complexes.} : {A metal alkylidene reacts with an alkene by [2+2] cycloaddition to a metallacyclobutane, whose cycloreversion exchanges alkylidene fragments and regenerates a metal carbene, as catalysed for example by Grubbs II or Schrock's Mo alkylidene complex.}

19. {How can Grubbs I and Grubbs II catalysts be prepared?} : {Grubbs I is made by converting RuCl2(PPh3)3 with phenyldiazomethane to a Ru benzylidene and replacing PPh3 with PCy3, whereas Grubbs II is obtained by replacing one PCy3 ligand of Grubbs I with an N-heterocyclic carbene generated from its imidazolinium salt.}

20. {Describe three of ROMP, RCM, ADMET, and CM with reaction sketches.} : {ROMP converts a strained cyclic olefin into [-CH=CH-(ring residue)-]n, RCM converts a diene into a ring alkene plus ethylene, and ADMET converts an alpha,omega-diene into an unsaturated polymer plus ethylene through repeated metathesis condensations.}

21. {Name at least two mechanisms for transition-metal-mediated olefin polymerisation.} : {Important mechanisms are coordination-insertion chain growth by the Cossee-Arlman pathway, metallocene-mediated insertion, and metal-carbene ring-opening metathesis polymerisation through metallacyclobutanes.}

22. {How does alkyne [2+2+2] cyclotrimerisation proceed, and what happens with nitriles?} : {Two coordinated alkynes oxidatively couple to a metallacyclopentadiene, a third unsaturated partner coordinates and inserts, and reductive elimination releases an aromatic ring, while replacing one alkyne with a nitrile gives a substituted pyridine rather than only benzene derivatives.}

## 4. Acid-Base, Organocatalysis, Phase Transfer, and Photochemistry

### Acid-base and phase-transfer catalysis

8. {Distinguish Bronsted and Lewis acid-base catalysis and explain general versus specific Bronsted acid catalysis.} : {Bronsted catalysis transfers a proton, as in H+-catalysed ester hydrolysis, whereas Lewis catalysis transfers an electron pair to an acceptor, as in BF3-activated carbonyl addition, and specific acid catalysis depends only on H3O+ after fast protonation while general acid catalysis involves proton donation by any acid in the rate-determining step.}

9. {Describe the principle of phase-transfer catalysis.} : {A phase-transfer catalyst such as a quaternary ammonium salt forms an ion pair with a reagent anion, carries it from an aqueous or solid phase into an organic phase where it reacts, and then shuttles back for another cycle.}

### Organocatalysis

31. {State advantages and disadvantages of organocatalysis.} : {Organocatalysts are often metal-free, air- and moisture-tolerant, inexpensive, easy to handle, and capable of asymmetric induction, but they frequently require higher loadings, react more slowly, have narrower substrate scope, and can be harder to recover than metal catalysts.}

32. {How does proline-catalysed enamine catalysis proceed?} : {Proline condenses with a carbonyl compound to an iminium ion, deprotonation gives a nucleophilic enamine that forms a stereocontrolled C-C bond with an electrophile, and hydrolysis releases the product while regenerating proline.}

33. {Distinguish covalent and non-covalent organocatalysis using a Bronsted-acid example.} : {Covalent organocatalysis forms a temporary bond such as an enamine between catalyst and substrate, whereas non-covalent catalysis activates through hydrogen bonding or ion pairing, for example HA + C=O <-> A<sup>-</sup>...H-O<sup>+</sup>=C lowers the barrier to nucleophilic attack and subsequent deprotonation regenerates HA.}

34. {Explain the asymmetric proline-derivative-catalysed tandem reaction shown in the source.} : {The diarylprolinol silyl ether forms an enamine from propanal for an enantioselective Michael addition to beta-nitrostyrene, then forms an iminium ion with cinnamaldehyde for a diastereoselective second Michael addition, after which intramolecular aldol condensation, dehydration, and hydrolysis furnish the chiral cyclohexene aldehyde and regenerate the catalyst.}

### Photochemical catalysis

38. {Define photocatalysis and identify the catalyst.} : {Photocatalysis uses light absorption by a photocatalyst, such as a semiconductor or molecular photosensitiser, to create an excited state that transfers an electron or energy to substrates and is regenerated after the reaction.}

39. {Why can the term photocatalysis be misleading under the strict definition of catalysis?} : {The term can be misleading because a catalyst should lower the activation barrier and be regenerated without changing equilibrium, whereas light supplies external free energy and can drive otherwise endergonic chemistry even though the photosensitiser itself cycles catalytically.}

## 5. Heterogeneous Catalysis and Porous Materials

### Surfaces, adsorption, and mechanisms

20. {Name three main differences between homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis.} : {Homogeneous catalysts share the reactant phase and usually offer uniform, highly tunable sites and strong selectivity but difficult separation, whereas heterogeneous catalysts occupy another phase, possess non-uniform surface sites, and are readily separated and recycled but often require harsher conditions.}

21. {Explain adsorption, desorption, physisorption, and chemisorption in gas-solid catalysis.} : {Adsorption transfers gas molecules to a solid surface and desorption returns them to the gas, with weak reversible van der Waals binding called physisorption and stronger site-specific bond formation with electronic rearrangement called chemisorption.}

22. {What are the differences and forces behind physisorption and chemisorption, with examples?} : {Physisorption is weak, often multilayer, non-specific van der Waals attraction exemplified by N2 on silica at low temperature, whereas chemisorption is stronger, usually monolayer and site-specific bonding exemplified by dissociative H2 adsorption on nickel.}

23. {Explain Langmuir-Hinshelwood and Eley-Rideal mechanisms and where they occur.} : {In Langmuir-Hinshelwood chemistry both reactants adsorb and react on the surface, as commonly proposed for CO oxidation on Pt, whereas in Eley-Rideal chemistry a gas-phase reactant attacks an adsorbed species directly, as in some hydrogen-atom recombination and oxidation reactions.}

24. {Name the key steps of heterogeneous gas-phase catalysis on a porous solid.} : {The sequence is bulk gas transport to the particle, external-film diffusion, pore diffusion, adsorption on active sites, surface reaction, product desorption, pore diffusion outward, and transport back into the bulk gas.}

25. {What does Henry's law describe for adsorbent materials?} : {At low pressure and low surface coverage Henry's law gives a linear adsorption isotherm, q = K<sub>H</sub>p, in which loading is proportional to gas partial pressure.}

26. {State the basic differences among Langmuir, Freundlich, and BET isotherms.} : {Langmuir assumes equivalent independent sites and saturating monolayer adsorption, Freundlich is an empirical power law for heterogeneous surfaces without a finite saturation limit in its normal range, and BET extends Langmuir concepts to multilayer physisorption and is used to determine surface area.}

### Automotive and oxidation catalysis

24. {Why is the automotive catalyst called a three-way catalyst, which metals are used, and what does each do?} : {It is a three-way catalyst because it simultaneously oxidises CO to CO2, oxidises hydrocarbons to CO2 and H2O, and reduces NOx to N2, using Pt and Pd mainly for oxidation and Rh especially for NOx reduction because of their respective adsorption and bond-activation strengths.}

25. {Describe the Mars-van Krevelen oxidation mechanism and the reactive oxygen species.} : {A substrate is oxidised by nucleophilic lattice O<sup>2-</sup> from a reducible metal oxide to form product and an oxygen vacancy with reduced metal centres, after which gas-phase O2 adsorbs, forms activated O2<sup>-</sup> or O<sup>-</sup> intermediates, and refills the vacancy to reoxidise the catalyst.}

26. {Which statements correctly describe a catalyst for heterogeneous CO oxidation with O2?} : {The correct statements are that late transition metals such as Pt or Pd dissociate O2 without needing to dissociate CO and that oxidation commonly follows a Langmuir-Hinshelwood pathway between adsorbed CO and adsorbed oxygen.}

### Catalyst preparation, deactivation, and porous solids

49. {Define heterogeneous-catalyst lifetime, give poison problems for two processes, and name at least two deactivation routes.} : {Catalyst lifetime is the useful operating time before unacceptable activity or selectivity loss, with sulfur poisoning iron in Haber-Bosch and lead or sulfur poisoning automotive noble metals as examples, while coking, sintering, poisoning, fouling, phase change, and leaching are common deactivation routes.}

50. {Outline precipitation-catalyst preparation and define calcination.} : {A soluble precursor is precipitated by controlling pH and temperature, then aged, filtered, washed, dried, shaped, and calcined, where calcination is heat treatment in a controlled atmosphere to remove volatiles and convert the precursor into a stable porous oxide or catalyst phase.}

51. {What are zeolites made of, and what are their special properties?} : {Zeolites are crystalline microporous aluminosilicate frameworks of SiO4 and AlO4 tetrahedra with charge-balancing cations, giving molecular-size pores, high area, ion exchange, tunable Bronsted acidity, thermal stability, and shape selectivity.}

52. {What are shape-selective catalysts, how are they used for p-xylene, what catalyst is used, and how are they prepared?} : {Shape-selective catalysts discriminate by pore size and diffusion, and modified ZSM-5 favours p-xylene in toluene methylation or xylene isomerisation because para-xylene diffuses out most readily, with ZSM-5 made by hydrothermal crystallisation of silica and alumina gels using a structure-directing agent followed by calcination and ion exchange.}

53. {How can zeolites be prepared?} : {Zeolites are prepared by hydrothermally crystallising an alkaline silica-alumina gel with mineralisers and often an organic structure-directing agent, followed by filtration, washing, drying, calcination to remove the template, and ion exchange to install the desired cation or H+ form.}

### Electronic structure, structure sensitivity, and syngas

57. {In the band model, what is the Fermi level and how do half-filled and highly filled d-bands differ in chemisorption?} : {The Fermi level is the highest occupied electronic energy at 0 K, and metals with roughly half-filled, higher-lying d-bands have many suitable empty and filled states and therefore bind adsorbates strongly, whereas late metals such as Pt have more filled, lower d-bands that generate greater antibonding occupation and generally weaker adsorption.}

58. {What are structure-sensitive reactions in heterogeneous catalysis?} : {Structure-sensitive reactions have rates or selectivities per exposed metal atom that depend on particle size, crystal face, steps, or ensembles because their transition states require a particular arrangement or coordination of surface atoms.}

59. {How is CO activated on a metal surface?} : {CO binds through carbon by sigma donation from its 5sigma orbital to the metal and pi back-donation from metal d orbitals into CO 2pi-star, weakening and lengthening the C-O bond, with bridge or hollow adsorption on electron-rich ensembles promoting stronger activation or dissociation.}

60. {What products arise from CO/H2 over Cu, Ni, Co, and Fe, and why?} : {Cu favours methanol because it stabilises oxygenated intermediates without facile C-O scission, Ni strongly hydrogenates dissociated carbon to methane, Co gives long-chain Fischer-Tropsch paraffins and olefins through efficient chain growth, and Fe gives Fischer-Tropsch hydrocarbons with more olefins plus water-gas-shift activity because it activates both CO and water-derived species.}

61. {Explain structure sensitivity and give an example of why surface-metal reactivity depends on overall structure.} : {Surface atoms at terraces, edges, and steps have different coordination and d-band properties, so reactions needing multi-atom ensembles change with morphology, as ammonia synthesis or CO dissociation is faster at iron step sites that bind and cleave N2 or CO more effectively than close-packed terraces.}

62. {Why can methane C-H cleavage occur at one surface atom while CO bond dissociation needs a stepped ensemble?} : {Methane can undergo local sigma-bond activation at a single undercoordinated atom that stabilises CH3 and H, whereas complete C-O cleavage requires simultaneous multi-centre binding and stabilisation of separate C and O fragments, which a stepped ensemble provides together with stronger back-donation.}

### Other heterogeneous phase combinations

73B. {Give two heterogeneous catalytic reactions that are not gas-solid reactions.} : {Examples are liquid-liquid phase-transfer catalysis, where a quaternary ammonium ion shuttles an anion between aqueous and organic phases, and liquid-solid hydrogenation, where a dissolved alkene and H2 react on a suspended Pd/C surface.}

## 6. Enzyme Catalysis and Biocatalytic Processes

### Enzyme structure, classes, and binding

26. {Name the six traditional enzyme classes and their reaction types.} : {The six traditional classes are oxidoreductases for redox reactions, transferases for group transfer, hydrolases for hydrolysis, lyases for non-hydrolytic bond cleavage or addition, isomerases for rearrangements, and ligases for energy-coupled bond formation.}

27. {Explain primary, secondary, and tertiary enzyme structure, prosthetic groups, and cofactors.} : {Primary structure is the amino-acid sequence, secondary structure comprises local helices and sheets, tertiary structure is the overall three-dimensional fold, prosthetic groups are tightly bound non-protein components, and cofactors are metal ions or organic coenzymes that supply chemical functions unavailable from amino-acid side chains.}

28. {What models describe substrate binding in enzymes?} : {The lock-and-key model treats the active site as pre-shaped for the substrate, whereas the induced-fit model says substrate binding drives a conformational change that aligns catalytic groups, and conformational-selection models add that the substrate selects a pre-existing active conformation.}

29. {What is enzyme inhibition, how is it represented, and what is it used for?} : {Enzyme inhibition is reduced activity caused by inhibitor binding, represented by competitive E + I <-> EI at the active site, uncompetitive ES + I <-> ESI, or non-competitive binding to E and ES, and it is used in metabolic regulation, medicines, pesticides, and mechanistic diagnosis.}

### Mechanisms and immobilisation

29. {What is carbonic anhydrase's role, mechanism, and central active-site feature?} : {Carbonic anhydrase rapidly interconverts CO2 + H2O and HCO3<sup>-</sup> + H<sup>+</sup> by using a Zn2+ centre coordinated by three [^2]histidines to lower the pKa of bound water, whose hydroxide attacks CO2 before bicarbonate displacement regenerates Zn-bound water.}

30. {Name and explain two ways to immobilise enzymes.} : {Enzymes can be covalently attached to a solid support through surface functional groups for low leaching and repeated use, or physically entrapped in a porous gel or capsule that retains the enzyme while allowing substrate and product diffusion.}

31. {What is a catalytic triad, and how does it catalyse ester hydrolysis?} : {A catalytic triad is a cooperative residue network such as Ser-His-Asp in serine proteases, where Asp polarises His, His deprotonates Ser for nucleophilic ester attack, the acyl-enzyme forms, and His-activated water hydrolyses it to release acid and regenerate Ser-OH.}

32. {What is the oxyanion hole for, and in which catalysis does it occur?} : {The oxyanion hole donates hydrogen bonds that stabilise the negatively charged oxygen of tetrahedral intermediates and transition states during serine-protease and related ester-hydrolysis catalysis.}

### Cofactor regeneration and industrial biocatalysis

52. {What are coupled-enzyme and coupled-substrate approaches to cofactor regeneration?} : {A coupled-enzyme system uses a second enzyme and sacrificial substrate to recycle a cofactor such as NADH, whereas a coupled-substrate system lets the same enzyme oxidise or reduce a cheap co-substrate in the reverse sense to regenerate that cofactor.}

53. {What is the HFCS process, its substrates and products, enzyme, flow scheme, and immobilisation mode?} : {HFCS production converts starch -> glucose syrup -> an equilibrium mixture enriched to about 42% fructose using immobilised xylose isomerase in packed columns, after which chromatographic enrichment and blending give commercial 55% fructose syrup while the bound enzyme remains for continuous reuse.}

54. {How does xylose isomerase convert glucose to fructose in HFCS?} : {At a dinuclear Mg2+ or Mn2+ active centre glucose binds and opens, metal-assisted proton transfers generate the proper alkoxide, a stereospecific hydride shift moves H from C2 to C1 while the carbonyl shifts from C1 to C2, and protonation plus ring closure releases fructose.}

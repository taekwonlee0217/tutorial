# Chemical Catalysis: Relevant Questions and Answers


## Green Chemistry and Sustainable Synthesis

1. {What are the twelve principles of green chemistry in condensed form?} : {They call for waste prevention, atom efficiency, safer substances, reduced toxicity and auxiliaries, low energy use, renewable feedstocks, minimal derivatization, catalysis, biodegradability, real-time monitoring, and accident prevention.}
2. {How is the E factor defined?} : {The E factor is the mass of all waste divided by the mass of desired product, with everything except the product counted as waste.}
3. {How do typical E factors vary by industrial sector?} : {Petrochemicals typically have E factors below 0.1, bulk chemicals 1-5, fine chemicals 5-50, and specialties or pharmaceuticals 20-100.}
4. {What economic and societal benefits can green chemistry provide?} : {It can reduce hazards, waste fines, capital and operating costs while enabling safer smaller plants, legal compliance, producer responsibility, and a better public image.}
5. {How is atom economy defined?} : {Atom economy is the molecular mass of the desired product divided by the total molecular mass of all products and rewards incorporation of reactant atoms into the target.}
6. {What is step economy?} : {Step economy seeks to construct the target framework and required functional groups in the fewest possible steps while rapidly increasing molecular complexity.}
7. {What is redox economy?} : {Redox economy minimizes nonstrategic or corrective oxidation and reduction steps that neither construct the framework nor establish required stereochemistry.}
8. {Why is the BHC ibuprofen process greener than the old process?} : {The BHC process uses fewer catalytic steps and produces less stoichiometric waste than the older multistep route.}

## Catalysis: History, Definitions, and Major Classes

9. {What everyday catalyst is emphasized in the introduction?} : {The [^10]three-way automotive catalytic converter is presented as one of the best-known catalysts in daily life.}
10. {What early catalytic observations preceded the term catalysis?} : {Examples include alcohol dehydration on heated clay, HCN cleavage on iron, hydrogen peroxide decomposition, combustion on platinum, and platinum-catalyzed alcohol oxidation or hydrogen ignition.}
11. {Who introduced the term catalysis and who supplied its classical kinetic definition?} : {Jöns Jacob Berzelius introduced the term in 1835, and Wilhelm Ostwald later defined catalysis kinetically.}
12. {What is the modern definition of a catalyst?} : {A catalyst increases reaction rate without being consumed or appearing in the final product and without changing the thermodynamic equilibrium position.}
13. {Why was the Haber-Bosch process historically decisive?} : {It enabled artificial nitrogen fixation when natural nitrate supplies were inadequate for agriculture and wartime demand.}
14. {Who developed and industrialized the Haber-Bosch process?} : {Fritz Haber developed the chemical procedure, Carl Bosch industrialized it in about four years, and Alwin Mittasch developed the iron catalyst.}
15. {What are the scale and energy burden of Haber-Bosch ammonia production?} : {The process produces roughly 120 million tonnes of ammonia annually and can consume about 1.1 percent of world energy production.}
16. {What belongs to the toolbox of catalytic science?} : {The toolbox includes catalyst classes, kinetics, thermodynamics, temperature effects, elementary steps, cycles, transition-state theory, performance metrics, and analytical methods.}
17. {How do homogeneous, heterogeneous, biological, and special catalysis differ?} : {Homogeneous catalysts share a phase with reactants, heterogeneous catalysts occupy another phase, enzymes provide highly specific biological active sites, and special catalysis includes photo-, organo-, phase-transfer, asymmetric, and electrocatalysis.}

## Catalytic Cycles, Thermodynamics, and Kinetics

18. {What does a catalytic cycle represent?} : {It represents the recurring elementary reactions that convert substrates to products while regenerating the active catalyst, alongside possible activation, decomposition, and side reactions.}
19. {Why can catalytic reactions be reversible?} : {A catalyst lowers kinetic barriers in both directions and does not alter the thermodynamic equilibrium.}
20. {Why is catalysis described as a kinetic phenomenon?} : {Catalysis changes the pathway and activation barriers of a reaction but not the overall free-energy difference between reactants and products.}
21. {Why are two-dimensional reaction-coordinate diagrams only approximations?} : {Real chemical energy landscapes are multidimensional surfaces containing multiple valleys, passes, saddle points, intermediates, and transition states.}
22. {What does the Arrhenius equation describe?} : {It expresses the temperature dependence of the rate constant through a pre-exponential factor and an activation energy.}
23. {What does transition-state theory add to kinetic analysis?} : {Transition-state theory relates rate to formation of the highest-energy activated complex and yields the Eyring equation using activation enthalpy and entropy.}
24. {What is the rate-determining step in a catalytic cycle?} : {It is the slowest elementary step and is usually associated with the largest relevant activation barrier.}

## Catalytic Performance and Active Species

25. {What is the turnover number?} : {TON is the amount of product per amount of catalyst and corresponds to the number of completed catalytic cycles per catalyst molecule or active site.}
26. {What is the turnover frequency?} : {TOF is TON per unit time and measures catalytic activity in reciprocal time units.}
27. {How are TON and TOF commonly reported for heterogeneous catalysts?} : {They are commonly normalized to an active site or to a mass of catalyst material.}
28. {What is the relationship between a precatalyst and the active species?} : {A precatalyst is converted under reaction conditions into the catalytically active molecular species or surface site.}


## Homogeneous Catalysis and Acid-Base Catalysis

29. {How do homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysts compare?} : {Homogeneous catalysts have well-defined modifiable sites and high selectivity but difficult separation and lower thermal stability, whereas heterogeneous catalysts are easier to separate and more robust but harder to characterize and tune.}
30. {How are homogeneous catalytic reactions broadly classified?} : {They include Brønsted acid-base, Lewis acid-base or nucleophilic-electrophilic, redox, coordination-complex, and organometallic complex catalysis.}
31. {What are Brønsted acids and bases?} : {A Brønsted acid donates a proton and a Brønsted base accepts a proton.}
32. {What distinguishes specific from general acid or base catalysis?} : {Specific catalysis depends only on hydronium or hydroxide, whereas general catalysis allows other acids or bases to participate in proton transfer.}
33. {What are classical examples of Lewis acid and nucleophilic catalysis?} : {Friedel-Crafts reactions use electrophilic Lewis acids such as aluminum chloride, while DMAP catalyzes [^1]acylations by nucleophilic catalysis.}
34. {What is Pearson's HSAB principle?} : {Hard acids preferentially bind hard bases and soft acids preferentially bind soft bases because of charge-controlled and orbital-controlled interactions.}

## Organometallic Bonding and Electron Counting

35. {Which bonding models are important in organometallic catalysis?} : {Important models include the 18-electron rule, valence-bond, VSEPR, molecular-orbital, Dewar-Chatt-Duncanson, crystal-field, and ligand-field theories.}
36. {Why is molecular-orbital theory needed for [^2]metal carbonyls?} : {It explains covalent donation and back-bonding in neutral metal carbonyls that a purely electrostatic ligand-field model cannot adequately describe.}
37. {What historical discoveries established organometallic catalysis?} : {Milestones include Zeise's salt, diethylzinc, nickel tetracarbonyl, metal carbonyls, hydroformylation, ferrocene, Ziegler-Natta polymerization, the Wacker process, cross-coupling, metathesis, and organocatalysis.}
38. {What does the 18-electron rule state?} : {Transition-metal complexes are often especially stable when metal d electrons plus ligand-donated electrons total 18, analogous to a noble-gas valence shell.}
39. {Why is the 18-electron rule also useful for oxidation-state assignments?} : {Ionic or covalent electron counting connects ligand charge and donation to the metal electron count and hence to its formal oxidation state.}
40. {What ligand families are common in metal-complex catalysis?} : {Common families include carbon ligands, [^3]phosphines, nitrogen donors such as bipyridine, [^4]N-heterocyclic carbenes, and [^5]chelating or [^6]pincer ligands.}
41. {What is ligand hapticity?} : {Hapticity, written eta, is the number of [^7]contiguous ligand atoms simultaneously bonded to a metal.}
42. {What complex types arise from ligand donation?} : {Donor-acceptor complexes use lone pairs, pi complexes use ligand pi orbitals, and sigma complexes use sigma-bond donation to the metal.}
43. {How does carbon monoxide bond to transition metals?} : {CO donates through its carbon lone pair and accepts metal electron density into its pi-star orbitals through back-bonding.}
44. {How does metal-to-CO back-bonding affect the CO stretching frequency?} : {Greater back-bonding weakens the C-O bond and lowers its infrared stretching frequency, as seen from cationic to neutral to anionic carbonyl complexes.}
45. {What does the Dewar-Chatt-Duncanson model explain?} : {It explains alkene and alkyne bonding as ligand-to-metal pi donation combined with metal-to-ligand pi-star back-donation.}

## Elementary Steps in Organometallic Catalysis

46. {What are the principal elementary steps of homogeneous organometallic catalysis?} : {They are oxidative addition, reductive elimination, ligand association and dissociation, nucleophilic attack, migratory insertion, and beta elimination.}
47. {What happens during oxidative addition?} : {A substrate bond adds across a metal center, normally increasing the metal oxidation state and coordination number by two.}
48. {What mechanistic pathways can oxidative addition follow?} : {It can proceed concertedly, by SN2 attack, ionically, or through radical steps.}
49. {What substrate and ligand trends are illustrated by oxidative addition to Vaska's complex?} : {Alkyl-halide reactivity follows I greater than Br greater than Cl, and more electron-donating phosphines accelerate the reaction.}
50. {What factors favor reductive elimination?} : {Cis disposition of the eliminating groups, steric crowding, high coordination number, and an electron-poor metal center generally favor reductive elimination.}
51. {What is migratory insertion?} : {A neutral coordinated ligand and an anionic metal-bound group combine in cis to form a new anionic ligand.}
52. {What is beta-hydride elimination?} : {It is the reverse-type process in which a metal alkyl forms an alkene and a metal hydride from a beta hydrogen.}
53. {How can precatalysts be activated?} : {Activation may occur by ligand dissociation, reduction with a base metal, reductive elimination, or hydrogenation of an unsaturated ligand.}
54. {Which variables control transition-metal catalysis?} : {The metal and oxidation state, ligands, solvent, energy source, and batch or flow reactor all control reactivity and selectivity.}


## Hydrogen Activation and Homogeneous Hydrogenation

55. {Which major reaction families are performed by homogeneous catalysts?} : {They include hydrogenation, [^8]hydrofunctionalization, cross-coupling, oligomerization, polymerization, [^9]C-H functionalization, and cycloaddition.}
56. {How can molecular hydrogen be activated by a metal complex?} : {It can be activated by oxidative addition, heterolytic cleavage or sigma-bond metathesis, or homolytic two-center cleavage.}
57. {Why are transition-metal hydrides chemically versatile?} : {Although their bonds are largely covalent, their structure can make them hydride donors or strong proton donors.}
58. {What is Wilkinson's catalyst known for?} : {RhCl(PPh3)3 was the first major homogeneous hydrogenation catalyst and is air-tolerant, ligand-exchangeable, and mechanistically well understood.}
59. {What are the main steps in Wilkinson-catalyzed alkene hydrogenation?} : {Ligand loss and alkene coordination are followed by hydrogen oxidative addition, hydride migratory insertion, and reductive elimination of the alkane.}
60. {What advantage does homogeneous hydrogenation offer?} : {It can provide high chemoselectivity and tolerance of other functional groups under mild conditions.}

## Transfer and Asymmetric Hydrogenation

61. {What is transfer hydrogenation?} : {Transfer hydrogenation uses a donor such as an alcohol or formic acid instead of gaseous hydrogen to reduce a substrate.}
62. {Why is asymmetric transfer hydrogenation valuable?} : {It converts prochiral ketones or imines into enantioenriched alcohols or amines.}
63. {What is the principle of asymmetric homogeneous hydrogenation?} : {A chiral ligand creates diastereomeric substrate-catalyst pathways with different energies, causing preferential formation of one enantiomer.}
64. {Which chiral ligand families are highlighted?} : {The lecture highlights DIOP, Chiraphos, DIPAMP, DuPhos, BPE, BINAP, SEGPHOS, Josiphos, and Monophos.}
65. {How does the Curtin-Hammett principle apply to asymmetric hydrogenation?} : {Rapidly interconverting catalyst-substrate diastereomers can give product ratios governed by the relative transition-state energies rather than ground-state populations alone.}

## Hydroformylation, Carbonylation, and Hydrofunctionalization

66. {What is hydroformylation?} : {Hydroformylation, or the oxo reaction, adds hydrogen and formyl groups from H2 and CO across an alkene to form aldehydes.}
67. {Which metals are important in hydroformylation?} : {Cobalt enabled the original Roelen process, while rhodium catalysts are now especially important industrially.}
68. {What do the Monsanto and Cativa processes produce?} : {They carbonylate methanol to acetic acid using rhodium and iridium catalysts, respectively.}
69. {What are hydrosilylation and hydroamination?} : {Hydrosilylation adds Si-H and hydroamination adds N-H across an unsaturated bond.}

## Cross-Coupling Reactions and Catalyst Design

70. {What are the canonical steps of a cross-coupling cycle?} : {Oxidative addition, transmetalation or nucleophile transfer, and reductive elimination connect two molecular fragments.}
71. {Why is transmetalation important?} : {It introduces the second organic group into the metal cycle and is often poorly understood and rate-determining.}
72. {Which reagents define major named cross-couplings?} : {Kumada uses organomagnesium, Stille organotin, Suzuki organoboron, Negishi organozinc, Hiyama organosilicon, Sonogashira alkynyl copper, Heck alkenes, and Buchwald-Hartwig amines.}
73. {What extra requirement enables Suzuki-Miyaura transmetalation?} : {A base forms an activated boronate ate complex or replaces palladium halide by an alkoxide that can undergo transmetalation.}
74. {Why is the Suzuki-Miyaura reaction widely used?} : {Organoboron reagents are versatile, functional-group tolerant, and useful from laboratory synthesis to industrial products such as boscalid.}
75. {What disadvantages accompany Stille coupling?} : {Organotin reagents are useful and often stable but are toxic and can cause difficult tin-containing waste and product contamination.}
76. {What side reactions can occur in cross-coupling?} : {Metathesis, homocoupling, and radical pathways can compete when alternative reaction paths are close in energy.}
77. {How does the Heck reaction functionalize alkenes?} : {After oxidative addition, an alkene inserts into a palladium-carbon bond and beta-hydride elimination releases the substituted alkene.}
78. {Why are aryl chlorides challenging but attractive coupling partners?} : {Their C-Cl bonds are stronger than C-Br or C-I bonds, but aryl chlorides are inexpensive, stable, and structurally diverse.}
79. {How do ligands enable coupling of aryl chlorides?} : {Bulky electron-rich ligands promote formation of unsaturated electron-rich metal centers that undergo oxidative addition to strong C-Cl bonds.}
80. {What does the Tolman cone angle measure?} : {It measures the steric demand of a phosphine ligand around a metal center.}
81. {What is the ligand bite angle?} : {The bite angle is the angle made by the two donor atoms of a chelating ligand at the metal, while the natural bite angle reflects ligand-backbone preference.}
82. {How is NHC steric demand commonly expressed?} : {It is commonly expressed as percent buried volume around the metal center.}
83. {Why does the metal-to-ligand ratio matter?} : {It determines catalyst speciation, coordination saturation, active-site availability, and therefore activity and selectivity.}
84. {How do palladium and nickel differ in coupling chemistry?} : {Nickel is less costly and can activate chloro- and even fluoroarenes, but its reactivity and selectivity are especially ligand dependent.}
85. {What is carbonylative cross-coupling?} : {It inserts CO into a metal-carbon bond during coupling to generate carbonyl-containing products.}
86. {How can the hazards of gaseous carbon monoxide be reduced?} : {Solid or liquid CO-releasing surrogates can generate the required carbon monoxide in situ.}
87. {What problem did Buchwald-Hartwig coupling solve?} : {It enabled catalytic coupling of aryl electrophiles with noncarbon nucleophiles, especially amines, to form C-N bonds.}

## Olefin and Alkyne Metathesis

88. {What is olefin metathesis?} : {A metal alkylidene exchanges alkene fragments through a metallacyclobutane-forming cycloaddition and cycloreversion sequence.}
89. {Why can entropy drive olefin metathesis?} : {Because broken and formed alkene bonds have similar enthalpies, favorable product-number or volatility effects can make the entropy term decisive.}
90. {How do Fischer and Schrock carbenes differ?} : {Fischer carbenes are electrophilic and use low-valent electron-rich metals with pi-acceptor ligands, whereas Schrock alkylidenes are nucleophilic and use high-valent electron-poor early metals.}
91. {What are the main variants of metathesis?} : {They include ring-opening polymerization, acyclic diene metathesis polymerization, ring-closing metathesis, cross metathesis, enyne metathesis, and alkyne metathesis.}
92. {Why is ring-closing metathesis important?} : {It is a general method for constructing carbocycles, heterocycles, and macrocycles in complex-molecule synthesis.}
93. {How is alkyne metathesis related to olefin metathesis?} : {It similarly uses a metal-carbene-type species and alternating cycloaddition and cycloreversion, but proceeds through metallacyclobutadiene intermediates.}

## Polymerization and Oligomerization

94. {What are the principal pathways for olefin polymerization?} : {They are radical, cationic, anionic, and coordination or metal-complex-catalyzed polymerization.}
95. {What is the Cossee-Arlman mechanism?} : {It describes Ziegler-Natta chain growth by alkene coordination followed by migratory insertion into a metal-carbon bond.}
96. {What is the SHOP process?} : {The Shell Higher Olefin Process oligomerizes ethylene with nickel catalysts and integrates isomerization and metathesis to make useful linear olefins.}
97. {What does the Schulz-Flory distribution predict for oligomerization?} : {It predicts that short even-carbon alpha-olefins dominate and product abundance decreases as chain length increases.}
98. {What are hemilabile ligands?} : {They are flexidentate chelates that reversibly release one donor to open a coordination site while retaining catalyst stability.}
99. {How can ligand design change ethylene oligomerization?} : {Small ligand changes can redirect a statistical product distribution toward selective metallacycle-mediated trimerization to 1-hexene.}

## Catalytic Cycloadditions

100. {Why are cycloadditions attractive in sustainable synthesis?} : {They form multiple bonds and ring systems with high atom economy, often in a single step.}
101. {How can a transition metal catalyze a cycloaddition?} : {It coordinates unsaturated partners as a template and promotes oxidative cyclization followed by product-forming steps.}
102. {What is the thermodynamic challenge of acetylene trimerization?} : {Formation of benzene is highly exothermic but has a large uncatalyzed activation barrier that a metal catalyst lowers.}
103. {What synthetic value does a catalytic 2+2+2 cycloaddition provide?} : {It constructs aromatic, heteroaromatic, carbocyclic, or heterocyclic rings in one step from three unsaturated units.}

## C-H Activation and Alkene Oxidation

104. {What is the biological precedent for C-H functionalization?} : {Cytochrome P450 enzymes use iron-oxo chemistry to oxidize otherwise unreactive C-H bonds.}
105. {Why is C-H activation difficult?} : {Unactivated C-H bonds are strong and poorly reactive, and their activation is often thermodynamically uphill unless coupled to a favorable follow-up reaction.}
106. {What general trends favor C-H activation?} : {Intramolecular and aryl C-H activation are usually easier, and heavier second- or third-row late transition metals often activate C-H bonds more readily.}
107. {What properties should a metal center have for C-H activation?} : {It should be coordinatively unsaturated, not sterically overcrowded, resistant to ligand cyclometallation, and possess filled orbitals able to interact with C-H sigma-star orbitals.}
108. {How do oxidative addition and sigma-bond metathesis activate C-H bonds?} : {Oxidative addition changes metal oxidation state by two, whereas sigma-bond metathesis exchanges bonds through a concerted pathway without that formal oxidation-state change.}
109. {What is orthometallation?} : {It is intramolecular activation of an ortho C-H bond directed by a coordinating group, often with nearly neutral entropy.}
110. {What does the Periana process achieve?} : {A platinum bipyrimidine catalyst activates methane in sulfuric acid to form methyl bisulfate, which can be hydrolyzed to methanol.}
111. {What is catalytic C-H borylation?} : {Rhodium or iridium catalysts replace aryl or alkyl C-H bonds with boronate groups that are versatile synthetic handles.}
112. {What is the Wacker process?} : {It oxidizes terminal alkenes such as ethylene with a palladium catalyst and a copper-mediated reoxidation system to give carbonyl compounds.}


## Catalyst and Ligand Synthesis

113. {How does homogeneous catalyst preparation differ from other catalyst manufacture?} : {It generally follows organic and organometallic synthesis, whereas chiral-ligand preparation and resolution can require much more work.}
114. {What is a classical preparation target in homogeneous catalysis?} : {Tetrakis(triphenylphosphine)palladium(0), Pd(PPh3)4, is a classical widely used precatalyst.}
115. {Which platinum-group-metal catalyst families are emphasized?} : {The handout surveys the preparation of palladium, rhodium, iridium, and ruthenium complexes from suitable metal precursors.}
116. {Why are chiral bisphosphines important?} : {They create asymmetric metal environments that convert prochiral substrates with high enantioselectivity.}
117. {What historically important chiral bisphosphine was introduced by Noyori in 1980?} : {BINAP was first synthesized and applied in catalysis by Noyori and coworkers in 1980.}
118. {How can modern BINAP synthesis avoid classical enantiomer separation?} : {An enantioselective or resolution-based synthetic sequence can establish axial chirality without separating diastereomeric palladium complexes at the end.}
119. {How are phosphine ligands made water soluble?} : {Sulfonate groups are introduced into the ligand framework to create water-soluble phosphines.}
120. {How can the BINAP scaffold be tuned?} : {Its phosphorus substituents and naphthyl backbone can be modified to change steric, electronic, solubility, and chiral properties.}
121. {How does BINAP dihedral angle affect selectivity?} : {A narrower dihedral angle was proposed and experimentally confirmed to increase enantioselectivity in suitable reactions.}
122. {What other chiral ligands are synthesized in the special handout?} : {The handout covers SEGPHOS, Q-Phos, Josiphos, pincer ligands, and BINOL-derived chiral cyclopentadienyl ligands.}
123. {Why can chiral Cp-complex synthesis be challenging?} : {Both metalation of the Cp ligand and isolation of a well-defined chiral precatalyst can be demanding.}
124. {How are N-heterocyclic carbene ligands commonly prepared from scratch?} : {They are commonly assembled as azolium salts and then deprotonated or metalated to generate the carbene complex.}


## Biocatalysis and Enzyme Fundamentals

125. {What future sustainability application is proposed for biocatalysis?} : {Enzymes may help decompose, remediate, and recycle persistent polymers.}
126. {What does beta-galactosidase illustrate?} : {It illustrates how an enzyme can accelerate glycoside hydrolysis far beyond simple acid or intramolecular catalysis.}
127. {What is an enzyme active site?} : {It is the localized three-dimensional region where substrates bind and catalytic chemistry occurs.}
128. {What reaction does acetylcholinesterase catalyze?} : {It hydrolyzes acetylcholine into choline and acetic acid or acetate, rapidly terminating neurotransmitter signaling.}
129. {What are the key early milestones of enzymology?} : {They include ancient fermentation, digestion by gastric juice, diastase action, Wöhler's urea synthesis, cell-free fermentation, the enzyme name, lock-and-key binding, urease crystallization, metabolic cycles, transition-state stabilization, and induced fit.}
130. {What are the six classical enzyme classes?} : {They are oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases, and ATP-coupled ligases.}
131. {What do oxidoreductases do?} : {They catalyze oxidation and reduction reactions such as alcohol oxidation, carbonyl reduction, epoxidation, and C-H oxidation.}
132. {What do transferases and hydrolases do?} : {Transferases move functional groups, while hydrolases cleave or form bonds involving esters, amides, nitriles, and epoxides through hydrolytic chemistry.}
133. {What do lyases, isomerases, and ligases do?} : {Lyases add to or form double bonds, isomerases rearrange molecules, and ligases form covalent bonds using ATP cleavage.}

## Protein Structure, Binding, and Enzyme Mechanisms

134. {What are the levels of protein structure?} : {Primary structure is amino-acid sequence, secondary structure includes helices and sheets, tertiary structure is the folded chain, and quaternary structure is assembly of multiple chains.}
135. {What stabilizes an alpha helix?} : {Intrachain hydrogen bonds between appropriately spaced carbonyl and N-H groups stabilize about 3.6 amino acids per turn while side chains point outward.}
136. {What structural feature characterizes a TIM barrel?} : {A central cavity framed by hydrophobic residues positions catalytic groups toward the cavity interior.}
137. {What are enzyme cofactors?} : {They are nonprotein components required for enzyme function that may participate in the reaction and must be regenerated.}
138. {What is special about coenzymes such as coenzyme A or NAD cofactors?} : {They undergo chemical change during catalysis and require regeneration, often by a second enzyme or coupled reaction.}
139. {Why are enzymes much larger than their active sites?} : {The surrounding protein precisely binds and orients substrates, tunes the environment, transmits conformational changes, and stabilizes transition states.}
140. {How do lock-and-key and induced-fit models differ?} : {Lock-and-key assumes a preorganized complementary site, whereas induced fit allows substrate binding to trigger a catalytically important conformational change.}
141. {How do enzymes accelerate reactions thermodynamically?} : {They preferentially stabilize the transition state and lower activation free energy without changing the reaction equilibrium.}
142. {What is the Sabatier principle for enzyme binding?} : {The transition state should bind strongly enough to be stabilized but not so strongly that turnover and product release are prevented.}
143. {How strongly does barrier lowering affect rate?} : {At 25 degrees Celsius, lowering the barrier by 10, 20, or 40 kJ per mole gives roughly 55-fold, 3,000-fold, or ten-million-fold acceleration.}
144. {Why are transition-state analogues potent enzyme inhibitors?} : {Their geometry and charge resemble the transition state, so enzymes can bind them much more strongly than substrates.}
145. {What are competitive, noncompetitive, and uncompetitive inhibition?} : {Competitive inhibitors displace substrate at the active site, noncompetitive inhibitors bind another site, and uncompetitive inhibitors bind only to the enzyme-substrate complex.}
146. {What is multivalency in enzyme recognition?} : {It is the combined effect of many individually weak contacts, including hydrogen bonding and other interactions, that creates strong selective binding.}
147. {How else can an enzyme lower a barrier besides direct transition-state stabilization?} : {It can destabilize the substrate, increase nucleophilicity, orient reactants, exclude solvent, and use long-range induced-fit effects.}
148. {How does ketosteroid isomerase catalyze double-bond migration?} : {An optimally placed aspartate acts as a general base while an oxyanion hole stabilizes the negative-charge-rich intermediate.}
149. {What did ketosteroid-isomerase mutants reveal?} : {Replacing active residues altered cavity size and water access, showing how precisely aligned hydrogen bonds stabilize the oxyanion intermediate.}
150. {How do enzymes distinguish enantiotopic or diastereotopic groups?} : {Three-point binding fixes substrate orientation so the active site can differentiate enantiomers or the re and si faces of a prochiral group.}

## Oxidoreductases and Oxygenation Enzymes

151. {How does alcohol dehydrogenase work in synthesis?} : {A zinc-containing active site and NAD-type hydride transfer reversibly interconvert alcohols and carbonyl compounds with high stereospecificity.}
152. {What characterizes P450 monooxygenases?} : {They use an iron heme center and molecular oxygen to generate a highly reactive oxidant that can perform stereoselective C-H oxidation.}
153. {What characterizes non-heme diiron monooxygenases?} : {They contain a carboxylate-bridged diiron site coordinated by glutamate and histidine residues and can oxygenate inert C-H bonds including methane.}
154. {What do alpha-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases require?} : {They require non-heme iron and alpha-ketoglutarate and incorporate both oxygen atoms into the products.}
155. {Why is prolyl hydroxylase biologically important?} : {It converts proline residues in collagen to 4-hydroxyproline, which stabilizes collagen's tertiary structure.}

## Hydrolases, Lipases, and Kinetic Resolution

156. {Why are hydrolases prominent in organic synthesis?} : {They are the largest enzyme class and can catalyze selective hydrolysis as well as reverse ester- and amide-forming reactions.}
157. {What is the catalytic triad of serine hydrolases used for?} : {It activates a serine nucleophile to form and hydrolyze an enzyme-bound acyl intermediate.}
158. {How do esterases, lipases, and proteases differ in substrate scope?} : {Esterases and lipases usually do not hydrolyze amides, whereas many proteases hydrolyze both amides and esters.}
159. {Why does equilibrium favor amides over esters?} : {Amides receive stronger resonance stabilization and are therefore generally more thermodynamically stable than esters.}
160. {How does papain hydrolyze an amide?} : {Its catalytic nucleophile forms an enzyme-bound acyl intermediate that is subsequently hydrolyzed to release products.}
161. {What distinguishes a lipase from an esterase?} : {Lipases are activated at an interface and undergo an opening conformational change, whereas esterases lack interfacial activation.}
162. {How are lipases used for kinetic resolution?} : {They acylate or hydrolyze one enantiomer faster because only that enantiomer fits the stereodifferentiating pocket well.}

## Enzyme Operating Conditions and Cofactor Regeneration

163. {How does pH affect enzyme activity?} : {Each enzyme has a usually near-neutral optimum, and more acidic or basic conditions alter catalytic-group protonation and can reduce stability.}
164. {How does temperature affect enzymes?} : {Rates initially follow Arrhenius behavior, but increasing instability and denaturation eventually cause activity to fall at higher temperatures.}
165. {What does first-order thermal inactivation mean?} : {A semilogarithmic decline in activity over time is linear and its slope gives the inactivation rate constant.}
166. {How can NADH or NADPH be regenerated?} : {Regeneration can be substrate coupled within one enzyme reaction or enzyme coupled through a second catalytic cycle.}
167. {How can hydrogen peroxide be supplied safely to peroxidases?} : {A coupled enzyme system can generate hydrogen peroxide in situ at a controlled rate.}

## Industrial Biocatalysis and Enzyme Engineering

168. {How is penicillin G converted on an industrial scale?} : {Penicillin acylase produces 6-aminopenicillanic acid, a core intermediate for semisynthetic antibiotics such as ampicillin and amoxicillin.}
169. {Why is stereochemistry crucial in the Tosoh-DSM aspartame process?} : {Only the L-configured amino-acid component gives the desired sweetness, so the biocatalytic step must be stereoselective.}
170. {Why is the Mitsubishi-Rayon acrylamide process notable?} : {Rhodococcus nitrile hydratase converts acrylonitrile to acrylamide at about 20 degrees Celsius with over 99.9 percent conversion and selectivity on a scale near 800,000 tonnes per year.}
171. {What is the largest biocatalytic process?} : {Immobilized glucose or xylose isomerase converts glucose to fructose for high-fructose corn syrup production.}
172. {How is high-fructose corn syrup made in the process shown?} : {A glucose syrup passes through reactors at about 55-65 degrees Celsius to give roughly 42 percent fructose, followed by purification and blending to the desired composition.}
173. {What methods immobilize enzymes?} : {Methods include covalent attachment to supports, entrapment, and cross-linking of precipitated enzyme aggregates into CLEAs.}
174. {What is directed enzyme evolution?} : {It iteratively creates genetic variants, screens or selects improved enzymes, and recombines beneficial mutations to evolve new catalytic properties.}
175. {What are artificial metalloenzymes?} : {They combine a protein environment with a synthetic metal catalyst to unite enzymatic selectivity with homogeneous organometallic reactivity.}


## Autocatalysis

176. {What is autocatalysis?} : {Autocatalysis is a reaction in which a product accelerates its own formation.}
177. {What classical reaction illustrates autocatalysis?} : {The acidic reaction of oxalic acid with permanganate, historically studied in titration kinetics, is a classical autocatalytic system.}
178. {What simple rate law describes A producing its own catalyst?} : {For initial concentration a and extent x, the ideal rate is proportional to (a-x)x, or to (a-x)(b+x) when a small catalyst concentration b is initially present.}
179. {Why does an autocatalytic concentration-time curve appear S-shaped?} : {The reaction begins slowly, accelerates as catalyst product accumulates, and slows again as reactant is depleted.}
180. {What is asymmetric autocatalysis?} : {It is a reaction in which a chiral product catalyzes its own formation and can amplify an initial enantiomeric imbalance, as in Soai chemistry.}

## Oscillating Reactions

181. {What is an oscillating chemical reaction?} : {It is a complex reaction in which intermediate and catalyst concentrations vary periodically instead of reaching a constant stationary value.}
182. {What is the first Lotka-Volterra rule?} : {Predator and prey populations oscillate periodically under constant conditions, with the predator maximum following the prey maximum.}
183. {How are Lotka-Volterra oscillations represented chemically?} : {Intermediate concentrations X and Y oscillate in time and trace closed phase-space paths whose shapes depend on initial conditions.}
184. {What state supports sustained chemical oscillation?} : {The system must be far from thermodynamic equilibrium while maintained in a nonequilibrium stationary regime.}
185. {What is the Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction?} : {It is a bromate-driven redox network whose intermediates and electrode potential oscillate through coupled feedback steps.}


## Haber-Bosch Ammonia Production

186. {What equilibrium factors favor ammonia formation from nitrogen and hydrogen?} : {Because 3 H2 plus N2 reversibly forms 2 NH3 exothermically with volume contraction, lower temperature and higher pressure favor ammonia.}
187. {What are ammonia's melting and boiling points?} : {Ammonia melts at about -77.76 degrees Celsius and boils at about -33.43 degrees Celsius.}
188. {What transport steps occur around a porous heterogeneous catalyst?} : {Reactants cross the external film, diffuse through pores, adsorb, react at active sites, desorb, and diffuse back through pores and film.}
189. {How is hydrogen-rich synthesis gas first generated for Haber-Bosch?} : {Methane and steam undergo nickel-catalyzed primary reforming at about 750-850 degrees Celsius and 2-4 MPa with a steam-to-methane ratio of 2.5-4.0.}
190. {What happens in the secondary reformer?} : {Air or oxygen oxidizes residual fuel above roughly 1,200 degrees Celsius and supplies nitrogen while lowering methane to below about 0.5 percent.}
191. {How is carbon monoxide converted during ammonia-gas preparation?} : {High-temperature Fe-Cr and low-temperature Cu-Zn water-gas-shift stages convert CO and water to CO2 and additional hydrogen.}
192. {How is carbon dioxide removed from ammonia synthesis gas?} : {It is removed chemically by carbonate or alkanolamine scrubbing, physically by solvents such as Rectisol or Selexol, or adsorptively by molecular sieves.}
193. {What pressure and temperature are used for ammonia synthesis in the handout?} : {The synthesis loop operates around 9-23 MPa and 350-530 degrees Celsius over alpha iron, giving about 25-35 percent ammonia per pass under stated conditions.}
194. {How do pressure and temperature affect ammonia equilibrium concentration?} : {At a fixed temperature increasing pressure raises equilibrium ammonia, while at a fixed pressure increasing temperature lowers it.}
195. {What is the composition of the Mittasch ammonia catalyst precursor?} : {It is mainly magnetite with roughly 2.5-4.0 percent Al2O3, 0.5-1.2 percent K2O, 2.0-3.5 percent CaO, up to 1.0 percent MgO, and 0.2-0.5 percent SiO2.}
196. {What roles do the Mittasch-catalyst additives play?} : {Alumina stabilizes structure, potassium oxide electronically promotes nitrogen dissociation, and calcium or magnesium oxides stabilize the macrostructure.}
197. {Which alternative ammonia catalysts are mentioned?} : {Historical osmium and modern supported ruthenium catalysts, cesium- or barium-doped ruthenium, and Hosono electrides are mentioned.}
198. {How is ammonia separated from the synthesis loop?} : {The gas is cooled so ammonia condenses near its low boiling point, while unreacted gas is recycled and inert purge gas is removed.}

## Ostwald Nitric Acid Production and Emissions Control

199. {What are the main stages of the Ostwald nitric-acid process?} : {Ammonia is catalytically oxidized to NO, NO is oxidized to NO2, and nitrogen oxides are absorbed in water to form nitric acid.}
200. {What reactions compete during ammonia oxidation?} : {Ammonia can form desired NO or undesired N2O and N2, with all three oxidation pathways strongly exothermic.}
201. {What operating conditions are used for catalytic ammonia oxidation?} : {Pt-Rh gauzes operate at about 800-950 degrees Celsius, 0.1-1.4 MPa, and millisecond contact time, giving about 96-98 percent NO yield.}
202. {How are NO and NO2 interconverted in nitric-acid production?} : {Secondary air oxidizes NO exothermically to NO2, which dimerizes to N2O4 at lower temperature and dissociates increasingly as temperature rises.}
203. {How does nitrogen dioxide form nitric acid?} : {NO2 or N2O4 reacts with water to produce nitric and nitrous acids, and nitrous acid disproportionates to nitric acid, NO, and water.}
204. {What conditions favor nitrogen-oxide absorption?} : {Low temperature and high pressure favor NO2 or N2O4 absorption and nitric-acid formation.}
205. {How are nitric-acid plants classified by pressure?} : {They are normal-pressure, medium-pressure at about 0.3-0.6 MPa, or high-pressure above 0.7 MPa, with single- or dual-pressure arrangements.}
206. {What is the advantage of a modern dual-pressure nitric-acid plant?} : {It optimizes ammonia oxidation and absorption separately and can recover about 11 GJ per tonne of HNO3-N as high-pressure steam.}
207. {How does rhodium affect Pt gauze performance?} : {An appropriate Rh content improves NO yield and mechanical behavior while influencing platinum-group-metal loss at high temperature.}
208. {How is platinum loss limited in nitric-acid plants?} : {Volatile PtO2 is trapped by palladium, which reduces it back to platinum while forming PdO that decomposes above about 750 degrees Celsius.}
209. {What is the state-of-the-art method for reducing NOx emissions?} : {Selective catalytic reduction uses ammonia over noble-metal or transition-metal-oxide catalysts to convert NOx into nitrogen and water.}
210. {What SCR reactions remove NO and NO2?} : {Ammonia reduces NO with oxygen, NO2 with oxygen, or a NO-NO2 mixture to N2 and H2O.}
211. {What catalysts are listed for SCR?} : {Noble metals and oxides such as V2O5, TiO2, CuO, and NiO are listed.}
212. {How can N2O emissions be reduced in nitric-acid production?} : {Primary gauze changes, secondary homogeneous or catalytic decomposition in the burner, and tertiary waste-gas decomposition or reduction can be used.}
213. {How are weak and strong nitric acid distinguished?} : {Weak acid reaches the 69.2 percent azeotrope, while strong acid above 98 percent is made by extractive distillation or direct high-pressure conversion of NO2 with water and oxygen.}
214. {What are major uses of weak nitric acid?} : {It is used for salts, nitrophosphate and ammonium-nitrate fertilizers, adipic acid and polymers, and explosives.}
215. {What are major uses of strong nitric acid?} : {It is used for nitration and esterification products including nitrobenzene, dinitrotoluene, nitroglycerin, nitrocellulose, diisocyanates, coatings, polymers, and explosives.}


## Xylose Isomerase

216. {Which metals are required by xylose or glucose isomerase?} : {The enzyme commonly requires two divalent metal ions, especially Mg2+, Mn2+, or Co2+, in its active site.}
217. {What roles do the metals play in xylose isomerase?} : {They coordinate substrate and water, stabilize negative charge, promote ring opening, organize catalytic residues, and facilitate the hydride-transfer isomerization.}
218. {What is a reasonable catalytic cycle for glucose-to-fructose isomerization?} : {Cyclic glucose binds and opens, metal-bound catalytic residues mediate proton and hydride transfer through an open-chain cis-enediol-like or hydride-shift pathway, fructose forms, closes, and dissociates while the active site is restored.}

## Heterogeneous Catalyst Performance, Deactivation, and Promotion

222. {What parameters govern a plug-flow catalyst bed?} : {Particle size and shape, mechanical stability, mass transfer, heat transport, adsorption, surface reaction, and desorption govern activity, selectivity, and stability.}
223. {How can heterogeneous catalytic activity be normalized?} : {Reaction rate can be reported per reactor volume, catalyst mass, surface area, or number of active sites under defined concentrations and temperature.}
224. {How is catalyst activity compared in screening?} : {Catalysts are compared at constant temperature and conversion or by the temperature needed to reach an identical conversion.}

## Heterogeneous Catalyst Materials, Supports, and Zeolites

231. {How are heterogeneous catalysts classified by active principle?} : {They include metals, metal or mixed oxides, solid acids and bases, and supported metals.}
232. {What is the difference among hydrogenation, hydrogenolysis, and hydrogen transfer?} : {Hydrogenation adds hydrogen to unsaturation, hydrogenolysis cleaves bonds with hydrogen, and hydrogen transfer moves proton or hydride equivalents between molecules.}
233. {What properties should an industrial solid catalyst possess?} : {It should be active, selective, poison resistant, thermally and mechanically stable, regenerable, reproducibly and sustainably manufactured, and economical.}

## Surface Sites and Adsorption

240. {How does d-band filling affect adsorption on transition metals?} : {Atoms such as C, O, N, and H generally bind more strongly to metals with less-filled d orbitals, while later metals adsorb less exothermically.}
241. {What is the difference between physisorption and chemisorption?} : {Physisorption is weak, reversible, and dominated by dispersion forces, whereas chemisorption forms stronger specific surface bonds and may activate or dissociate molecules.}
242. {What surface features can act as active sites?} : {Terraces, steps, edges, vacancies, and small surface clusters provide sites with different coordination and reactivity.}

## Adsorption Isotherms and Surface-Reaction Mechanisms

243. {What does an adsorption isotherm describe?} : {It relates surface coverage to adsorbate equilibrium pressure at constant temperature.}
244. {Which adsorption isotherms are emphasized?} : {The lecture emphasizes the Langmuir, Freundlich, and Brunauer-Emmett-Teller isotherms.}
245. {What assumptions underlie the Langmuir isotherm?} : {It assumes a fixed number of equivalent sites, monolayer adsorption, one adsorbate per site, and no lateral adsorbate interaction.}
246. {What does the BET isotherm measure?} : {It extends adsorption to multilayers and is widely used to determine the specific surface area of porous solids.}
247. {How do Langmuir-Hinshelwood and Eley-Rideal mechanisms differ?} : {Langmuir-Hinshelwood has both reactants adsorb before reacting, whereas Eley-Rideal has a gas- or solution-phase reactant attack an adsorbed species.}
248. {Why can pressure help identify a surface mechanism?} : {The two mechanisms produce different rate dependences on reactant pressure and surface coverage.}


## Characterization of Heterogeneous Catalysts

249. {Why are modern analytical methods crucial in heterogeneous catalysis?} : {Active sites are nonuniform and can change under reaction conditions, so complementary bulk, surface, microscopic, and operando measurements are required.}
250. {What do temperature-programmed methods reveal?} : {They monitor gas consumption or product release during controlled heating to probe reduction, oxidation, desorption, reaction, and catalyst preparation effects.}
251. {What does X-ray diffraction reveal?} : {XRD identifies ordered bulk crystal phases, estimates crystallite size, and can follow structural transitions in situ.}
252. {How does XPS work and what does it measure?} : {Monochromatic X-rays eject core electrons whose kinetic energies reveal surface elements, composition, oxidation states, impurities, and lateral uniformity.}
253. {Why is XPS surface sensitive?} : {Photoelectrons have a limited escape depth, so mainly atoms near the outer surface contribute to the spectrum.}
254. {Why is magic-angle spinning used in solid-state NMR?} : {MAS narrows broad anisotropic solid-state lines and allows local environments such as different silicon-aluminum sites in zeolites to be resolved.}
255. {What information do TEM and SEM provide?} : {Electron microscopy reveals catalyst morphology, particle size, dispersion, surfaces, and microstructure.}


## Structure Sensitivity and Surface Ammonia Synthesis

256. {What is structure sensitivity in heterogeneous catalysis?} : {It means reaction rate depends on nanoparticle size, exposed facets, steps, or other site geometry, unlike structure-insensitive reactions.}
257. {What does a volcano curve express?} : {Maximum activity occurs at intermediate adsorption strength because weak binding prevents activation and strong binding blocks turnover or product release.}
258. {What are the main surface steps in Haber-Bosch ammonia synthesis?} : {Nitrogen and hydrogen adsorb and dissociate, surface nitrogen is sequentially hydrogenated to NH, NH2, and NH3, and ammonia desorbs.}
259. {Why does iron surface structure matter in ammonia synthesis?} : {Nitrogen dissociation requires ensembles of appropriately coordinated iron atoms that are more abundant on particular facets, steps, and defects.}
260. {Why is Haber-Bosch condition selection a compromise?} : {Low temperature favors equilibrium ammonia but slows kinetics, while high pressure favors ammonia but increases compression and reactor costs.}

## Heterogeneous Oxidation and Automotive Catalysis

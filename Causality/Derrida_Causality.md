### Section 1: Why Derrida’s philosophy emerged

1. {Why did Derrida develop deconstruction?} : {He found that philosophical systems often depend on distinctions they cannot consistently maintain. Deconstruction developed as a way to examine how a text’s own reasoning complicates its governing assumptions, especially claims about origins, identity, and immediately available meaning.}
    
2. {Why did Derrida engage deeply with earlier philosophers instead of constructing an entirely independent system?} : {Because the language and concepts available to him were already inherited from philosophical traditions. Criticism therefore had to work through that inheritance, exposing its limits while acknowledging its continuing usefulness.}
    
3. {Why did phenomenology become an important starting point for Derrida?} : {Phenomenology examines how things become meaningful in experience. Derrida pursued its questions about consciousness, time, and ideal objects, but challenged the possibility that meaning could become completely present to consciousness without mediation or difference.}
    
4. {Why did Husserl’s account of ideal objects lead Derrida toward writing?} : {An ideal object, such as a mathematical theorem, must remain identifiable beyond its discoverer’s experience. Its transmission requires repeatable expressions and potentially written records. This makes material inscription significant to the historical survival of supposedly purely ideal meaning.}
    
5. {Why did Heidegger influence Derrida’s approach?} : {Heidegger showed how inherited philosophical vocabulary can conceal assumptions about Being and time. Derrida adopted the practice of interrogating that inheritance, while also questioning whether Heidegger’s own language escaped the assumptions he criticized.}
    
6. {Why did Derrida’s work emerge through engagement with structuralism?} : {Structuralism explained elements through their relations within systems. Derrida pursued this relational insight while questioning whether a system could have a stable center or boundary exempt from the relations that constitute its other elements.}
    
7. {Why should Derrida’s intellectual development not be explained by one historical cause?} : {His work emerged through several intersecting engagements: phenomenology, structural linguistics, literature, psychoanalysis, and the history of metaphysics. A single explanation would obscure the different problems that motivated different texts.}
    

Primary text for the relation between inherited concepts and structuralism: [“Structure, Sign, and Play.”](https://www.literatureoftheamericas.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Structure-Sign-and-Play.pdf)

### Section 2: Why presence, speech, and origins became targets

8. {Why did Derrida criticize the “metaphysics of presence”?} : {He argued that influential philosophical traditions privilege what appears immediately available: a present object, a conscious intention, or an original meaning. His criticism asks whether such presence already depends on relations to what is absent, past, or anticipated.}
    
9. {Why has speech often been treated as more authoritative than writing?} : {A speaker seems present to explain an utterance and guarantee its intention. Writing can circulate without its author. This contrast encourages the belief that speech gives direct access to meaning while writing merely represents it at a distance.}
    
10. {Why does Derrida question that apparent immediacy of speech?} : {Spoken words must also be recognizable and repeatable. They depend on conventions, temporal sequences, and differences from other words. Consequently, a speaker’s presence cannot eliminate all the mediation traditionally associated with writing.}
    
11. {Why does Derrida call the privileging of speech “phonocentrism”?} : {The term identifies the authority granted to the voice as an apparent vehicle of immediate thought. Derrida examines how hearing oneself speak can produce an impression of complete self-presence, although speech still unfolds through time and inherited signs.}
    
12. {Why is phonocentrism connected to “logocentrism”?} : {Logocentrism seeks an authoritative foundation of meaning, such as reason, truth, or an originating thought. Speech becomes privileged when it appears closest to that foundation. Phonocentrism is therefore one important form of a broader philosophical orientation.}
    
13. {Why did Derrida expand the meaning of “writing” into “arche-writing”?} : {He needed a term for the spacing, differentiation, and repeatability that make both speech and conventional writing possible. Arche-writing names these structural conditions; it does not claim that humans historically invented written script before speaking.}
    
14. {Why does Derrida question pure origins?} : {An origin becomes identifiable through distinctions, repetition, and subsequent interpretation. What appears to precede all mediation can therefore depend on mediation for its identity. His question concerns the purity of origins, rather than denying that events have historical beginnings.}
    

Relevant primary work: [_Of Grammatology_.](https://www.press.jhu.edu/books/title/11199/grammatology)

### Section 3: Why meaning involves différance and traces

15. {Why did Derrida develop the term “différance”?} : {He wanted to connect differentiation with temporal deferral. Meaning arises through relations among signs and through processes that unfold over time. The term resists treating these two dimensions as separate operations.}
    
16. {Why does différance contain an “a” instead of an “e”?} : {In French, the altered spelling is visible but inaudible. This makes writing register a distinction that speech cannot convey by pronunciation alone, challenging the assumption that writing simply reproduces distinctions already available in the voice.}
    
17. {Why does meaning depend on differences?} : {A sign becomes identifiable through its relations to other signs. For illustration, “legal” gains meaning through distinctions involving “illegal,” “lawful,” “permitted,” and “legitimate.” Its meaning cannot be explained by considering it completely alone.}
    
18. {Why does meaning involve deferral?} : {Understanding an expression involves other expressions, earlier uses, and anticipated developments. Meaning is produced through these relations rather than delivered as an entirely self-contained object in a single instant. Deferral does not mean that ordinary understanding never occurs.}
    
19. {Why did Derrida introduce the concept of the “trace”?} : {A present element is shaped by its relations to elements that are not presently there. “Trace” names this constitutive relation to otherness. It is more fundamental than an ordinary surviving mark left by something previously complete.}
    
20. {Why is the present not completely self-contained?} : {Experience involves retaining what has just passed and anticipating what may follow. For illustration, hearing a melody requires earlier notes to remain relevant to the current note. An isolated instant would not yield the same experience.}
    
21. {Why is différance not simply a first cause?} : {A first cause would usually be imagined as an identifiable source producing subsequent effects. Différance names relations through which identifiable sources and effects become possible; turning it into an independent originating entity would repeat the foundation-seeking gesture Derrida questions.}
    

Primary text: [“Différance.”](https://web.stanford.edu/class/history34q/readings/Derrida/Differance.html)

### Section 4: Why deconstruction proceeds through close reading

22. {Why does deconstruction examine binary oppositions?} : {Oppositions such as speech/writing or nature/culture often organize arguments while assigning authority to one term. Examining them reveals how a text establishes its values and whether the privileged term depends on what it subordinates.}
    
23. {Why is simply reversing an opposition insufficient?} : {Declaring writing superior to speech would preserve the original hierarchy’s structure. Deconstruction may temporarily reverse a hierarchy to expose its force, but it also asks how the opposition itself becomes unstable.}
    
24. {Why does Derrida look for internal tensions rather than merely contradicting an author?} : {An external objection can leave a text’s organizing assumptions untouched. An internal reading shows how the argument relies on something its explicit claims exclude, making the difficulty arise from the text’s own operation.}
    
25. {Why can a footnote, metaphor, or apparently minor passage matter?} : {An argument may manage a major difficulty in a seemingly marginal location. The passage matters when its function reveals a dependence or exclusion central to the argument—not simply because it is obscure.}
    
26. {Why does Derrida resist defining deconstruction as a fixed method?} : {Different texts organize their tensions differently. A universal procedure would prejudge what reading should discover. Deconstruction requires careful attention to particular arguments, vocabularies, genres, and contexts rather than a mechanical sequence of steps.}
    
27. {Why does deconstruction require both fidelity and intervention?} : {The reader must reconstruct an argument accurately before demonstrating how it exceeds its stated intentions. Fidelity supplies the evidence; intervention develops implications that the text may not explicitly acknowledge.}
    
28. {Why does deconstruction not mean destroying a text?} : {Its force depends on sustained engagement with the text’s reasoning. It makes inherited concepts available for reconsideration by showing their complexity, rather than simply discarding the works in which they appear.}
    
29. {Why is identifying a contradiction not always enough for a deconstructive reading?} : {A contradiction might be an ordinary mistake. A stronger reading explains why the difficulty is connected to the argument’s conditions of possibility—for example, why what the text excludes also enables its central claim.}
    

These methodological distinctions are illustrated in [“Structure, Sign, and Play.”](https://www.literatureoftheamericas.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Structure-Sign-and-Play.pdf)

### Section 5: Why the supplement and pharmakon matter

30. {Why did Derrida focus on Rousseau’s concept of the “supplement”?} : {A supplement can mean an additional enhancement or a substitute supplying something missing. Derrida uses this doubleness to examine whether what Rousseau presents as naturally complete already requires supplementation.}
    
31. {Why does supplementation complicate the distinction between an original and an addition?} : {If an addition is necessary for the original to function, the original was not simply complete beforehand. What appears secondary can help constitute what appears primary. This challenges a clean separation between essential identity and external assistance.}
    
32. {Why does writing become an important example of supplementation?} : {Writing is often described as something added to speech to preserve or extend it. Yet speech already depends on repeatability and differentiation. Writing exposes conditions that the supposedly independent original also needs.}
    
33. {Why did Derrida examine the word “pharmakon” in Plato?} : {In the discussion of writing, the Greek term carries associations with both remedy and poison. Derrida examines how this doubleness complicates the attempt to assign writing an unambiguously subordinate or harmful role.}
    
34. {Why can translating pharmakon as only “remedy” or only “poison” change an argument?} : {Each translation selects one direction of a semantic tension. That selection can make the philosophical opposition appear more settled than the Greek wording permits. Translation therefore participates in interpretation rather than merely transporting an unchanged content.}
    
35. {Why is the pharmakon more than an example of an ambiguous word?} : {Its significance comes from its role in an argument about memory, knowledge, and writing. The same practice can preserve memory while encouraging dependence on external reminders. The tension concerns the argument’s structure, not merely vocabulary.}
    

The supplement is examined in [_Of Grammatology_](https://derridas-margins.princeton.edu/library/derrida-de-la-grammatologie-1967/gallery/p-234/); the pharmakon is examined in “Plato’s Pharmacy,” discussed in the [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s account of deconstruction.](https://iep.utm.edu/deconstruction/)

### Section 6: Why repetition changes communication

36. {Why did Derrida develop the concept of “iterability”?} : {A sign must be repeatable to function as a recognizable sign. Derrida emphasizes that this repeatability also permits its use in different circumstances, linking recognizable identity with the possibility of alteration.}
    
37. {Why can an utterance exceed its author’s intention?} : {Its words belong to conventions that others can reuse. For illustration, a private sentence can later become courtroom evidence or a public slogan. The original intention remains relevant but cannot control every subsequent use.}
    
38. {Why can no context guarantee complete closure of meaning?} : {A sign can be cited in another context, and the boundaries of a context can themselves become objects of interpretation. Context constrains meaning, but no description can permanently exhaust every possible relevant relation.}
    
39. {Why does Derrida examine theatrical or quoted language when discussing speech acts?} : {Ordinary performative utterances rely on repeatable conventions. Their ability to appear in quotation or performance is connected to that repeatability, rather than an entirely external accident that leaves their structure untouched.}
    
40. {Why is a signature both singular and repeatable?} : {It identifies a particular signer, yet must remain recognizable across different inscriptions and circumstances. The repeatability enabling authentication also permits imitation, showing how a condition of success can create a vulnerability.}
    
41. {Why does the possibility of misunderstanding belong to communication itself?} : {Communication works because signs can circulate beyond a particular speaker and moment. That circulation also makes reinterpretation possible. The capacity enabling successful transmission therefore cannot be completely separated from the risk of failure.}
    

Primary text: [“Signature Event Context.”](https://web.stanford.edu/class/history34q/readings/Derrida/Derrida_Signature_Event.html)

### Section 7: Why meaning remains constrained despite openness

42. {Why does Derrida’s account not imply that every interpretation is equally good?} : {Interpretations must answer to wording, argumentative structure, historical circumstances, and relevant evidence. Openness permits further questioning; it does not erase the distinction between a supported reading and an arbitrary assertion.}
    
43. {Why does the statement commonly translated as “there is nothing outside the text” not simply deny reality?} : {Its point concerns access to meaning: appeals to reality do not provide an automatically interpretation-free standpoint. Bodies, events, and institutions remain consequential, but their significance cannot be isolated from contextual relations.}
    
44. {Why can meanings become relatively stable?} : {Shared conventions, repeated practices, and institutional settings constrain interpretation. For illustration, a train timetable supports dependable coordination despite possible errors or later revisions. Practical stability does not require an absolutely final meaning.}
    
45. {Why can Derrida still make arguments if he questions ultimate foundations?} : {An argument can establish distinctions and support conclusions without claiming an unquestionable starting point. Derrida’s challenge concerns absolute guarantees, not the usefulness of reasoning, evidence, or careful disagreement.}
    
46. {Why should “undecidability” not be understood as ordinary indecision?} : {Ordinary indecision may disappear after obtaining more information. Derridean undecidability concerns situations where competing demands cannot be settled completely by an existing rule, although a decision still has to be made.}
    

The contextual character of meaning is developed in [“Signature Event Context”](https://web.stanford.edu/class/history34q/readings/Derrida/Derrida_Signature_Event.html); undecidability and decision are developed in [“Force of Law.”](https://deontologistics.co/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/derrida-force-of-law.pdf)

### Section 8: Why deconstruction leads toward justice and responsibility

47. {Why did ethical and political questions become increasingly explicit in Derrida’s work?} : {Questions about exclusion, authority, and relations to others already had ethical implications. His later writings developed these implications through particular problems involving law, responsibility, hospitality, and political institutions. This was a development rather than an uncomplicated abandonment of earlier concerns.}
    
48. {Why does Derrida distinguish law from justice?} : {Law requires general rules and enforceable procedures. Justice demands attention to particular people and circumstances that no general rule can exhaust. The distinction explains why lawful decisions remain open to criticism and revision.}
    
49. {Why can mechanical rule-following fail to produce justice?} : {A rule groups cases together, while each case contains particular circumstances. Applying the rule responsibly requires judgment about those circumstances. For illustration, equal penalties can have unequal significance when relevant conditions differ.}
    
50. {Why does a responsible decision involve uncertainty?} : {If a complete rule already determined the outcome, the act would resemble executing a program. Responsibility arises where judgment must engage competing demands without possessing a guarantee that the decision is entirely just.}
    
51. {Why must decisions still occur despite undecidability?} : {Waiting for complete certainty can itself harm people and evade responsibility. A decision must interrupt deliberation while remaining answerable to the reasons considered and the consequences that follow.}
    
52. {Why is justice described as undeconstructible?} : {Justice names the demand that motivates criticism and exceeds any settled legal formulation. It is not an existing institution exempted from scrutiny. Its role is to prevent current arrangements from declaring themselves finally sufficient.}
    

Primary text: [“Force of Law.”](https://deontologistics.co/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/derrida-force-of-law.pdf)

### Section 9: Why gifts, forgiveness, and hospitality involve paradoxes

53. {Why does Derrida question whether a pure gift is possible?} : {A gift appears to require giving without return. Yet gratitude, obligation, prestige, or the giver’s self-satisfaction can introduce a return. The difficulty arises because recognizing an act as a gift can reconnect it to exchange.}
    
54. {Why does this argument not make ordinary generosity worthless?} : {It examines the demanding concept of giving without recompense. Ordinary giving can still help others, while reflection on its mixed motives prevents the giver from assuming that generosity is entirely free of self-interest.}
    
55. {Why does Derrida connect forgiveness with the unforgivable?} : {If forgiveness applies only after an offense becomes excusable or adequately compensated, it risks becoming something else. Unconditional forgiveness confronts wrongdoing precisely where ordinary justification cannot make it acceptable.}
    
56. {Why must forgiveness be distinguished from reconciliation or legal pardon?} : {Reconciliation repairs a relationship, while legal pardon changes an institutional consequence. Forgiveness concerns a different relation to wrongdoing. These processes can intersect, but achieving one does not automatically accomplish the others.}
    
57. {Why does unconditional hospitality conflict with conditional hospitality?} : {Unconditional hospitality would welcome another without prior qualification. Actual hospitality requires arrangements involving space, resources, rights, and responsibilities. These arrangements make welcome practicable while also placing limits on it.}
    
58. {Why does Derrida preserve both forms of hospitality?} : {Unconditional welcome challenges restrictive arrangements, while conditional practices provide concrete ways to receive people. Their tension demands continuing judgment about how institutions can become more hospitable under particular circumstances.}
    
59. {Why does Derrida repeatedly discuss “the impossible”?} : {He examines demands that cannot be fully realized by a predictable procedure: a pure gift, unconditional welcome, or entirely just decision. Their impossibility challenges complacency and calls for invention; it does not simply recommend inactivity.}
    

Relevant texts: [_Given Time_](https://web.english.upenn.edu/~cavitch/pdf-library/Derrida_Given_Time.pdf), [“On Forgiveness”](https://w2.uib.no/filearchive/derrida-on-forgiveness-part-1.pdf), and [“The Principle of Hospitality.”](https://www.scribd.com/document/366589477/DERRIDA-The-principle-of-hospitality-pdf)

### Section 10: Why history, inheritance, and democracy remain unfinished

60. {Why did Derrida develop “hauntology”?} : {The present is affected by people, promises, and injustices that are no longer—or not yet—present. The figure of the specter helps explain this influence without treating history as a collection of fully self-contained present moments.}
    
61. {Why did Derrida return to Marx after the collapse of Soviet communism?} : {Declarations that Marxism had become irrelevant did not eliminate exploitation, inequality, or the demand for justice. Derrida argued for critically inheriting emancipatory possibilities without treating every Marxist doctrine or political regime as authoritative.}
    
62. {Why is inheritance an active responsibility?} : {An inheritance contains conflicting possibilities rather than one unified instruction. Receiving it requires selecting, interpreting, and transforming what is passed down. Fidelity therefore involves judgment rather than simple repetition.}
    
63. {Why does Derrida speak of “democracy to come”?} : {No existing democracy completely fulfills its promises of equality and openness. The phrase keeps those promises available for criticism and transformation, including responsiveness to people excluded by current arrangements.}
    
64. {Why does “democracy to come” not mean postponing democracy until the future?} : {Its demand bears on present decisions. Institutions must be improved now while remaining open to challenges they cannot fully anticipate. The future names this openness rather than a scheduled final political condition.}
    
65. {Why can democracy endanger itself through “autoimmunity”?} : {A democracy may restrict liberties to protect itself, damaging the openness it claims to defend. Conversely, openness can enable forces hostile to democracy. Autoimmunity names this tension within practices of self-preservation.}
    

The historical and political questions are developed in [_Specters of Marx_](https://eclass.upatras.gr/modules/document/file.php/PHIL2037/__Specters_of_Marx__The_State_of_the_Debt__The_Work_of_Mourning__amp__the_New_International__Routledge_Classics_.pdf); democracy and autoimmunity are discussed in the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s Derrida entry.](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/derrida/)

### Section 11: Why archives, animals, and identity broaden the inquiry

66. {Why does an archive do more than preserve information?} : {An archive selects, classifies, stores, and regulates access to records. These operations help determine what later counts as evidence. For illustration, excluding certain correspondence can shape an institution’s future account of its own history.}
    
67. {Why does archival technology affect historical understanding?} : {Different recording and retrieval systems make different things preservable and discoverable. A searchable digital collection permits inquiries that an inaccessible paper collection may obstruct. The medium therefore influences the future uses of what is recorded.}
    
68. {Why did Derrida question the general category “the animal”?} : {The singular category groups immensely different living beings together as humanity’s opposite. Derrida asks how this simplification supports claims that humans uniquely possess certain capacities and deserve unrestricted authority over other creatures.}
    
69. {Why does an animal’s gaze matter philosophically?} : {Being seen by a particular animal disrupts the position of the human as sole observer and classifier. Derrida uses this encounter to reconsider vulnerability and the relationship between philosophical categories and another living being.}
    
70. {Why does questioning human exceptionalism not require denying all differences between species?} : {The issue is whether many complex differences can be reduced to one absolute boundary. More careful differentiation can challenge human superiority without claiming that humans and other animals possess identical capacities.}
    
71. {Why does Derrida question whether someone completely owns their language?} : {Language comes through other people, institutions, and inherited conventions. A person speaks through something they did not independently create. This complicates the connection between linguistic identity, belonging, and possession.}
    

Relevant works: [_Archive Fever_](https://thetelos.org/archive-fever-mal-darchive-jacques-derrida/), [_The Animal That Therefore I Am_](https://fordhampress.com/the-animal-that-therefore-i-am-hb-9780823227907.html), and the discussion of language and identity in the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy.](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/derrida/)

### Section 12: Why Derrida’s philosophy remains difficult and contested

72. {Why does Derrida sometimes write through unusual spellings, wordplay, and disrupted forms?} : {He examines how wording and textual form participate in philosophical arguments. Altering them can expose distinctions that conventional expression conceals. Such experiments must be evaluated through what they reveal in a particular reading.}
    
73. {Why did his work become influential in literary studies?} : {Its attention to rhetoric, ambiguity, genre, and relations between stated intentions and textual effects provided ways to reconsider literary interpretation. These approaches were subsequently adapted by critics, sometimes beyond Derrida’s own formulations.}
    
74. {Why do critics question deconstruction’s rigor?} : {Some argue that its readings overgeneralize from linguistic ambiguities or fail to establish clear standards for judging interpretations. Assessing these objections requires examining specific arguments rather than assuming that either all deconstruction or all criticism is uniformly sound.}
    
75. {Why do critics question deconstruction’s political usefulness?} : {Its resistance to final foundations can seem to leave action without definite guidance. A Derridean response emphasizes situated judgment and revisable institutions, but the question of how these yield concrete political choices remains a substantial debate.}
    
76. {Why should Derrida not simply be classified as a relativist or nihilist?} : {He challenges absolute guarantees while continuing to distinguish careful argument from arbitrary interpretation and responsible action from indifference. Nevertheless, whether his account adequately grounds these distinctions is open to philosophical criticism.}
    
77. {Why can deconstruction also be applied to Derrida’s own writings?} : {His vocabulary also depends on inherited distinctions and can acquire new exclusions or rigidities. Exempting it from questioning would turn an approach concerned with limits into an unquestionable authority.}
    
78. {Why does a causal understanding of Derrida require examining conditions as well as events?} : {Historical explanations ask how his work emerged. Philosophical explanations ask what enables meaning, identity, and responsibility. Keeping these questions distinct prevents treating a structural dependence as a historical sequence or reducing an argument to its author’s circumstances.}
    
79. {Why can this guide provide broad understanding without a final mastery of Derrida?} : {His concepts acquire their force through detailed readings of particular works. Definitions supply orientation, but understanding develops by following those arguments, comparing interpretations, and examining where the proposed distinctions succeed or remain disputed.}
    

For the range of interpretations and controversies, see the [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s Derrida entry.](https://iep.utm.edu/jacques-derrida/)

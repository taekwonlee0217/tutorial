Heinrich Müller is happy. Vacation at last! He has waited a long time for his vacation.

Three weeks without stress. No company, no lab, no experiments. Nothing!

Only sun, beach, and palm trees.

The day after tomorrow it starts. A fantastic trip: two weeks in the Dominican Republic, club hotel, 'Tuttobello', all packaged, all-inclusive. He doesn't need to do anything, everything is perfectly organized there:

Every morning at nine o'clock continental breakfast on the terrace, at eleven a short tennis match with his wife, in the afternoon sunbathing by the swimming pool, after that a massage, and in the evening club entertainers provide good entertainment. Everything well-planned, but without stress.

He has just been to the travel agency in Munich and picked up the papers. Now he is driving home to Rosenheim, a small town in Bavaria, a good half hour from Munich. His wife is expecting him at one o'clock for lunch. He looks at his watch: three minutes before half-past twelve.

Just before the highway, he sees a young man by the side of the road. A hitchhiker.

Normally, Heinrich Müller never picks anyone up. You can never know. These people often look so uncivilized with their long hair and unshaven faces. They would probably smoke in his car without asking, and he doesn't like that at all.

But because Heinrich Müller is in such a good mood today, he wants to make an exception.

So he stops, rolls down the window, and says: “I can give you a ride. But I'm only going as far as Rosenheim.”

The young man smiles and says: “Thank you. Rosenheim is better than nothing.”

He puts his backpack in the trunk and gets in.

They drive on, and Heinrich Müller thinks: The guy isn't untidy at all, he looks quite pleasant.

“Where are you trying to get to today, if I may ask?” he inquires.

“To Italy, if possible,” the hitchhiker answers.

“Ah. And if it doesn't work out?”

“That doesn't matter either. Then I'll travel to Italy tomorrow. Or the day after tomorrow.”

“Ah. And in Italy, I suppose you want to go to the sea?”

“No, not this time. I'd like to go to Bologna, and then to Siena and Perugia, if possible.”

“Mhm. And what are you going to do in all those places, if I may ask?”

“Well, in Bologna I have friends, in Siena I mainly want to see the cathedral, and in Perugia there's a great jazz festival in June.”

“So you are interested in art history and jazz music?”, asks Heinrich Müller.

“Yes,” said the hitchhiker, “that too. But the concerts, the churches, and museums—maybe that's not the most important part. The whole atmosphere in these towns is simply great. The people, the bars, the restaurants. Especially in Perugia. Do you know Perugia?”

“No, I was only in Rome once for a weekend. And twice in Milan for a conference,” answers Heinrich Müller, and then continues asking:

“Tell me, do you do all of this by hitchhiking?”

“I don't think so. I'll also take a train or a bus sometimes. Or I'll walk a bit.”

“But traveling like that must be very tiring and impractical. I mean, all the waiting. And the uncertainty...”, says Heinrich Müller.

“Yes, that's true. But it's also very exciting. Sometimes you're lucky, sometimes you're just unlucky. But in any case, you experience a lot. Every day something new happens. And you get to know a lot of people. I speak a little Italian, and this way I can practice it really well.”

“I see,” says Heinrich Müller once more.

A short time later, they are already in Rosenheim.

Heinrich Müller stops at a traffic light. The hitchhiker gets out, thanks him, and says:

“Well, whenever you have a vacation, why don't you drive to Perugia too? It's just six or seven hours from here. You'll see, it's worth it! It's truly an experience!”

“Yes, yes,” nods Heinrich Müller, “that's not a bad idea. Goodbye!”

Five minutes later he arrives home. He has a thoughtful look on his face.

His wife notices it and immediately asks:

“Everything went smoothly at the travel agency, right, Heinrich?”

“Yes, yes,” he answers softly, “of course, I have all the papers.”

“Fantastic! But you don't seem happy at all. Is something wrong?”

“No, no,” says Heinrich Müller slowly, looking out the window, “everything is in perfect order, my dear. Really.”

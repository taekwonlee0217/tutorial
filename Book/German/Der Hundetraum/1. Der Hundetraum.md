Waldemar looks at the clock on the wall. My God! Almost six o'clock already. He has found the whole afternoon so boring. He didn't feel like coming right from the start. Coffee and cake at Doris and Hermann's. Herbert and Sabine are here too, of course. And Bruno and Beate anyway. These friends of his wife. Terrible! And then Raimund too, her hairdresser.

Waldemar wanted to stay at home, lie on the sofa, and simply do nothing. Later maybe go for a short walk. Why not? But not to Doris and Hermann's!

“Of course you're coming with me, darling,” Linda said at half-past two, “you can't let me go alone, and at Doris's there is always such a good cake. You've always liked it.”

Oh God! That cake. Always the same cake. Chocolate cake. Ugh! And after that, a sweet liqueur. Apricot or raspberry. That's even worse. He has never drunk sweet liqueur.

“Don't make such a face, honey. We'll be back home at six o'clock. I promise. Two little hours, you can manage that,” his wife said at half-past two, put on her lipstick, and once again put on far too much perfume. Apricot, like the liqueur, gross! “And hurry up a bit, we're leaving in ten minutes!”

It's not just the cake. You don't have to eat the cake. It's these people, these faces, these conversations. Always the same people, the same faces, the same conversations. Always the same topics: children and vacation, vacation and children. The children at school, the vacation in Tenerife, Raimund's new motorhome. Our Klaus, piano lessons, Lena, last-minute, Sebastian, seasick...

How was it in Greece? Nice and very cheap. But Bruno got sick, didn't he, Bruno? Yes, Beate. And Mallorca? Also good, though quite expensive. But we got really tanned, right, Herbert? Yes, Sabine.

It goes on like this for at least three hours. And all of it with photos, of course. Hundreds of photos. Look! Ah, I see. Look here! Oh, okay. Until everything has been told. Everything about the vacation and everything about the children. When everything has been said, the jokes come.

Waldemar looks at the clock again. Around six o'clock, the jokes usually come. Always the same jokes, of course. Or almost the same. Still, Waldemar finds the jokes to be the best part.

But today there aren't even jokes. Today it's dreams' turn. Who started it? Probably Doris. Suddenly Doris told a dream. And everyone listened, completely fascinated.

“So, I dreamed that we were coming back from a trip. The trip was wonderful, but of course, you also look forward to home a little bit: to the good dark bread, old friends, German television.”

Everyone nods. Yes, yes, of course.

“So I go into the bakery, but there's no bread. All the shelves are empty. And then I call my friends, but no one picks up the phone, nobody is there. I turn on the TV, wanting to watch the news, but all the people look so foreign, and I don't understand a word.”

“Oh,” everyone cries, “that's terrible!”

“Yes, and then Hermann suddenly says that we're still on safari. Four more weeks! Simply terrible.”

That was Doris. And now the others have to tell a dream too. First it's Linda's turn.

She is standing in a room. It's a shop or a boutique. Suddenly a man walks up to her. He has a pair of scissors in his hand and looks dangerous. He is already very close. She wants to run away, but she can't move. She panics and screams. The man laughs and suddenly doesn't look dangerous anymore: She knows him. It's...

How embarrassing, Waldemar thinks, how can anyone dream such foolish things and then even tell everyone about them?

Then it's Hermann's turn. He tells something about a soccer match. A final in a big stadium. Hermann scores three goals. The crowd roars: Go Hermann, score another goal! He is the big star.

What nonsense! Of all people, Hermann with his beer belly.

Waldemar wants to go home. But now everyone is looking at him. Full of expectation. He has found the whole afternoon terrible. And now he has to tell a dream, too! Well, just you wait...

“Well,” he begins, resting his arms on the table, “if it has to be. But I warn you. This is not a dream, this is a nightmare. You see, I once dreamed that I was a dog.”

“A dog?”, everyone asks.

“Yes,” says Waldemar, “I am a handsome, clever dog, and Linda is my mistress. She is very proud of me. That's why she always takes me along. To the office, shopping, to the hairdresser, to her female friends. And everyone always says what a good dog I am. Linda smiles then, says 'I know,' and puts her hand on my head. And I'm always lying under the table, always having to wait, and there are always terrible sweets.

It goes on like this every day. For many dog years. But one day, I don't feel like it anymore. It can't go on like this. That's it. Basta.”

“Oh,” everyone says, “and what happens?”

“We go to her friends once again. I don't want to come along, I want to stay at home. But I have to come along. As always.

I think we are here, at Doris and Hermann's. And all of you are there too. As always. But this time, it's particularly boring for me. There aren't even any jokes. Everyone just talks and talks and talks. And I lie on the floor and have to wait and wait.

Then somebody calls me. I'm supposed to come and eat another piece of cake, down there under the table. I get up and walk to the table, very slowly. Everyone looks at me, and someone says again: 'What a good dog!'

But this time I don't lie down under the table, and I don't eat any cake either. I jump onto the table, paws right into the whipped cream, and start barking—loudly and angrily. I bark like crazy. Cups and plates fall to the floor and shatter.

I am protesting—protesting against this damned cake, protesting against these boring afternoons, protesting against this terrible coffee gossip, against my mistress, against her awful perfume, against this smirking hairdresser. In short: I am protesting against this damned dog's life!”

“Ohhh!” everyone around the table cries out.

Waldemar pauses and looks around the table triumphantly. Everyone looks at him, nobody speaks, everyone is completely perplexed. Total silence.

“What...”, Doris finally says quietly, “what is wrong with Waldi? He has never barked before.”

“And he has never jumped on the table either,” the hairdresser whispers, “he always lay so politely on the floor.”

“Well,” says Linda, “I don't know either. He's been so restless all day today. Maybe he's sick. I think it's better if we go home now. Too bad, the dreams were so interesting. Oh well, next week again. Come on, Waldi, come to Mommy, hop!”

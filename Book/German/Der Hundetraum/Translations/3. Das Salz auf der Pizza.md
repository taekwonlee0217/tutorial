On this Friday evening, Peter comes home late. He is tired; he has worked the whole week. But tonight he is not only tired, he is also sad. Really depressed. He has already been in Berlin for five months now, but he still hasn't found any friends. And he was so excited about this city. Finally out of the small town and into the big metropolis.

Yet it started off well: He immediately found this apartment in the apartment building in Berlin-Schöneberg. He enjoys his new job at the computer company, too. And Berlin really is a wonderful city.

There is so much to see, hear, and experience: cafés and cinemas, cabaret and theater, grand opera houses, famous orchestras, and fantastic galleries and museums. Plus bars and pubs for every taste, open day and night. Going out 24 hours a day, around the clock—that only exists in Berlin.

But it's not just the nightlife and culture in this city that are incredible. Berlin is ideal for nature lovers, too. Just a few kilometers from the city center, there are lovely lakes where you can go boating, swimming, and sunbathing. Or you can go to one of the large parks, Hasenheide for example. Everyone meets there. People read, chat, lounge on the lawns, play soccer, or have a picnic. In the evenings, there are big barbecue parties.

And on the weekend, people drive out into the countryside or into the Grunewald forest. Even the Baltic Sea is only three to four hours away by car.

But all of that is only fun when you're not alone.

It really is absurd, Peter thinks. You leave your village because you know everything and everyone there, and then in the big city you're unhappy because it's so anonymous and impersonal.

Sure, he has gotten to know a few people already: the employees at the company, for example. His boss is very pleasant, and his colleagues are nice and helpful, too. Two or three times a week, they go to the cafeteria together at noon. But lunch break only lasts 45 minutes, and they only talk about the company. After work, at five o'clock, everyone goes home quickly. Most of them have families.

Besides, many of his colleagues live in completely different neighborhoods. Fifteen, twenty kilometers—you can easily spend an hour traveling by subway. The distances here in Berlin are extremely large.

But of course, there are other ways to meet people. You can, for example, go out alone and hope that something happens.

Or you can go to a nightclub and flirt a little. But how to start?

Ask something. But what?

*What time is it?* That's childish. He has a watch himself.

*Do you have a cigarette?* Oh come on, he doesn't even smoke.

*Do you want to dance with me?* My God, nobody dances as a couple in clubs anymore. All nonsense! And he isn't the type for those things anyway.

So maybe a sports club. Sports connect people, they say.

Peter isn't particularly athletic, but he quite likes playing volleyball. However, only every now and then when he feels like it, not organized and mandatory three times a week like it normally is in clubs. Peter already has enough stress and appointments with work.

But does it have to be a sports club? He could also sign up for an evening course or a workshop. Those are very trendy right now. In the newspaper, there is a huge selection: learning languages or making music, painting or vegetarian cooking, playing chess or meditating. Everything you could possibly imagine.

But does he have to cram Russian now just because he wants to meet a few people? Or act in a theater, or drum on bongos?

And who knows what the people in the course are like. Maybe rather boring. Then week after week you sit in a room with people you don't even find that pleasant, having to learn something you're no longer particularly interested in, just because you've already paid. And organized group dynamics—Peter doesn't need that.

He wants to have friends. There must be another way!

Actually, he has already made contact. Almost every evening. At home.

He has discussed all sorts of things with all sorts of people. He has exchanged information. But he hasn't really gotten to know anyone, hasn't even seen anyone.

Peter is an IT specialist and knows his way around new media. Surfing the internet, joining chat rooms—no problem for him.

But there is no face, there is no voice. That isn't a real conversation. That is just communication.

Besides, he didn't come to Berlin to sit in front of the computer every night.

So he tried something else—and it didn't work out. That's why he is so sad tonight.

He actually always thought it was completely stupid, but now he tried it himself: He placed an ad in the newspaper, in the Berlin magazine *City*.

Of course, he didn't write "Lonely Aquarius seeks Pisces" or any such nonsense. He finds that ridiculous. He wrote that he is new here and looking for people who feel like going out occasionally in the evening or taking a trip on the weekend. Quite simple.

Yesterday the ad appeared in *City*. And yesterday evening someone called right away. Senta from Berlin-Charlottenburg. A psychology student. She said that in her free time she often goes to the gym and is currently urgently looking for a dance partner. Especially salsa.

Actually, those weren't his hobbies and not his style of music either. But he said that they could definitely grab a coffee together and get to know each other.

“Yes, why not?” Senta said, and they made a date. For today at half-past six, at Café *Mimikry* on Kantstraße.

After work, Peter went straight there. He was pretty nervous. A blind date—that was new for him. But he was really looking forward to it.

Finally a rendezvous.

Then he waited—ten minutes, twenty minutes, half an hour. First he drank a coffee because he wanted to keep a clear head. Then, already after seven, he ordered a beer, and then another one. He waited until eight o'clock, but she didn't come. Of course, he finally thought, I'm probably not the only one she called, and someone else surely likes dancing salsa!

So now Peter has come home again, late and sad and tired. Tired from work, tired from the beer, and tired from waiting. The fridge is empty; he didn't buy anything. He had been thinking of going to a restaurant with this Senta.

Friday evening, usually the best moment of the week. And he sits there, on his sofa, staring at the wall.

What now? Surf the internet? Watch the Friday crime show on TV?

No, doesn't feel like it. First put on some music and rest. The Mexican record he brought back from his trip last year. That's how he can relax best. Sleep a little, forget everything, and call the pizza delivery service later.

Suddenly, the doorbell rings.

Was he dreaming? But no, it really rang. A colleague? They have his phone number, but not his address. The pizza delivery? But he hasn't even called them yet. Family? Old friends? Rather unlikely.

Peter gets up, goes to the door, and opens it.

In front of him, in the dark hallway, stands a young woman he doesn't know. *Senta*, he thinks, *that must be Senta! Did she come after all?*

“Senta?” he asks, full of expectation.

The young woman laughs.

“Senta? Nah, I'm not Senta. My name is Franka. I'm your neighbor, I live a few doors down.”

“Oh, I see,” Peter says, disappointed.

“I don't want to disturb you, I just wanted to ask if you have any salt. I'm cooking right now and just noticed that I'm out of salt. The shops are already closed. Maybe you could...”

This Franka looks incredibly pleasant.

“Of course,” Peter says, “come on in.”

“Thank you, that's very nice.”

Peter grins.

“I barely have anything in the kitchen, but I have plenty of salt.”

She looks around the apartment.

“You've furnished it really nicely here!”

“Well,” Peter says while filling a cup with salt in the kitchen, “a lot of it is from the flea market. How long have you been living here?”

“Oh, a good two years. And you?”

“Almost five months.”

“That long already? I've never seen you. I always thought nobody lived here.”

“Yeah,” Peter says, “it's funny here in Berlin. You live door to door and don't know each other.”

“Yeah,” the young woman says, “that's really sad. But I guess that's how it is in big cities.”

Peter holds the cup with the salt in his hand. He hesitates for a moment.

“Tell me, can I offer you something? A glass of wine? I have a nice Rioja from Spain here.”

She shakes her head.

“That's very nice, but I have to get back to the kitchen.”

She takes the salt and walks to the door. There she turns around once more.

“By the way, have you eaten yet?”

“No,” answers Peter, “I just got home and was going to call the pizza delivery service later.”

“Look,” Franka smiles, “that works out well. The salt is actually for a pizza. I invited a friend over. Why don't you just come on over if you don't have other plans? In about half an hour.”

“I don't really have plans, but I don't want to intrude either.”

“Nonsense, you're not intruding. We'd be glad to have you. And there's plenty of food. Don't worry.”

“Alright then,” Peter says, “I'd love to come.”

“Great then,” says Franka. “By the way, I think we can use the informal *'du'*. Agreed?”

“Of course,” says Peter, “can I bring anything else?”

“No, the salt was the most important thing. Oh, right, that nice Rioja, if you like.”

“That goes without saying,” says Peter, “so, see you in a bit.”

Franka is already back out in the corridor, but she turns around one more time.

“And bring that record along. I was going to knock on another door earlier, but then I heard that music. I really like it.”

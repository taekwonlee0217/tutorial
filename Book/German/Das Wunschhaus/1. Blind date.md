## 1

"Another glass of sparkling wine?" he asks.

"Yes," Sarah smiles. "Why not?"

That's already the third one, she thinks, but it doesn't matter.

Who would have thought it! That it would be so easy, so funny, so exciting.

In the past, she had only shaken her head at things like this. Personal ads, blind dates — what nonsense! But then her girlfriends had talked about it again and again, saying that nowadays it was perfectly normal and that nothing bad could really happen. A kind of game, but entirely respectable. Much better than sitting around at home or waiting for a miracle in a café.

Above all, her friends had told her, everything was in her own hands. Nobody would come up to her and start talking to her uninvited. Instead, she could choose an interesting personal ad herself. She would write a short letter, and that would be all.

And then? You meet, get to know each other a little. Perhaps it will just be a pleasant evening, perhaps a little flirtation, perhaps a good friendship, perhaps something more.

Of course, the chances of something more developing — true love or something like that — are rather small. Even Sarah's friends had admitted that. And naturally, an evening like this could sometimes be boring and disappointing, even downright embarrassing.

So what? It's only one evening. What's the big deal?

One thing that must always be exciting is the very first moment. Like a little scene from a movie. Glances and props.

How will I recognize you? How will you recognize me? A crowd of people, and somewhere among them, that mysterious unknown person, X.

And then the mystery is solved...

## 2

The advertisement had been rather matter-of-fact, almost neutral. But Sarah had preferred that to all those boastful stories.

Code word: Gentleman.

She had particularly liked that. Somehow, it sounded promising.

The telephone conversation yesterday had been short. The Gentleman hadn't even told her his real name.

The "script" for their meeting hadn't been particularly original either.

He would wait for her at the Café Odeon, he had decided.

No problem.

With a newspaper, the Frankfurter Allgemeine.

No problem.

At the bar.

No problem.

That was everything. No problem.

Sarah hadn't found it very romantic. Somehow, she had imagined flowers, but apparently those days were over.

She had also wanted to say something about herself on the phone, about her hair, her dress, but the Gentleman hadn't wanted to know such details.

No problem.

No problem, no problem. What was that supposed to mean? Couldn't the guy say something nice for once?

The whole thing had sounded rather routine, like a business appointment.

For a moment, Sarah had considered canceling the whole thing. It was ridiculous, complete nonsense.

But she didn't even have his telephone number.

So she would just have to go through with it.

That night, she had even had a bad dream.

She walks into the bar. A thousand men are sitting behind a thousand newspapers. A thousand pairs of eyes look at her, quickly examining her from head to toe, then staring back into their newspapers.

She walks up to the counter and asks whether the bar stool is free.

In response, a chorus of a thousand male voices:

"Yes, yes, no problem."

Terrible!

## 3

And now everything has turned out completely differently. Right from the very first moment.

There weren't a thousand men at the bar, only two or three. The mystery had been solved immediately.

He had been sitting right by the entrance and had looked up from his newspaper. A glance, a friendly smile.

"Is this seat free?" she had asked, because at that moment she couldn't think of anything better to say.

"But of course," he had replied, removing his jacket from the bar stool.

"May I?"

He had even helped her take off her coat. That hadn't happened to her in a long time.

A real gentleman, indeed.

Then he had folded up his newspaper and placed it on the counter.

"But that's not the Frankfurter Allgemeine," she had remarked.

"No," he had replied. "It's the Süddeutsche."

Again, that smile.

"Is that a problem?"

"No, no," she had quickly answered. "No problem."

Then she had briefly bitten her lip.

He had also introduced himself immediately: Klaus Buchner.

At last, an end to all those code words and pseudonyms.

And now they were having a really good conversation.

None of that psychological babble she had feared, no talk about being single or complicated relationships.

They were talking about everything under the sun: films, travel, their jobs.

Klaus was a wonderful storyteller, full of anecdotes and stories.

There was plenty to laugh about.

But that wasn't all. He also listened, asked questions, and showed genuine interest.

## 4

Suddenly, a ringing sound. His mobile phone.

He apologizes and turns slightly to the side.

Sarah takes a sip of wine and leans against the bar.

She feels so comfortable.

Life can be so beautiful.

When you meet the right people, when you're in good company.

She looks around.

The Odeon has filled up. A loud, lively crowd, couples, groups, a good atmosphere, good music.

Opposite Sarah, on the other side of the bar counter, sits a woman, elegantly dressed, sipping a cocktail, waiting for something, ready for a wonderful evening.

Perhaps her anticipation will soon turn into disappointment, Sarah thinks.

She knows that feeling only too well.

She too has often waited in vain for something.

Next to the woman sits a man, partly hidden from view, wearing a suit and tie.

With one hand, he nervously drums his fingers on the counter, while with the other he impatiently flips through a newspaper.

A glance at his watch, a reach for his cocktail.

That's how things are, Sarah reflects.

Those two could be talking to each other right now. They would probably even get along quite well.

But they won't speak to each other. They won't get to know one another.

Nobody has written a personal ad. No contact, no arrangement to meet.

Sarah finds it really sad that things can't happen differently.

That people can hardly ever simply start talking to one another spontaneously.

That you can only meet men like Klaus in such a strange way.

She thinks once again about the whole business: the advertisement, the letter, the strange telephone conversation.

No problem. No problem.

He hasn't said that once all evening.

Perhaps now she could tell him that she had found it rather strange.

She's curious about what he would say.

## 5

At that moment, Klaus turns back toward Sarah and apologizes once again.

"These mobile phones," he says. "All this stupid talking on the phone, always at the wrong moment."

Now or never! Sarah thinks, gathering all her courage.

"Tell me, why are you actually so different on the phone? So distant, so serious?"

Klaus looks at her in surprise, then smiles again.

"Well, my colleague was somewhere out on the street. I could hardly understand him, and then there was all the noise in here..."

My God, doesn't he understand what I mean?

Sarah says nothing more.

Perhaps it would be better not to bring it up at all...

Her gaze falls once again on the two people opposite her.

The woman is now smoking a cigarette. The man looks at his watch again, picks up his glass, and finishes his drink.

"Was I really that unfriendly?" Klaus asks.

"Oh no, forget about it!"

Sarah shakes her head, without taking her eyes off the man on the other side of the bar.

He places a banknote on the counter, folds up his newspaper, and tucks it under his arm.

"I'll switch it off," she hears Klaus say. "Then nobody can disturb us anymore."

Sarah stares at the newspaper and holds her breath.

There is no doubt.

It's a Frankfurter Allgemeine.

The man comes around the bar counter, pushing his way determinedly through the crowd.

There's hardly any room. The bar is very crowded.

He stops in front of Sarah.

She's sitting in his way. He can't get past.

He looks at her unfriendly.

"Excuse me, can I get through? I'm in a hurry."

"Sorry," she says, moving to the side.

And then...

Then she smiles.

"Go right ahead. No problem."

End of "Blind Date."

## Understanding the ending

The central twist is that Sarah has accidentally spent the evening with the wrong man.

The real Gentleman is the impatient man carrying the Frankfurter Allgemeine. Klaus, the charming man she has been talking to, was simply another customer at the bar.

The irony is that Sarah had been convinced that modern relationships required personal advertisements and carefully arranged meetings. Yet she has just experienced exactly what she thought was nearly impossible: a wonderful spontaneous encounter.

The repeated expression kein Problem also changes meaning. At first, she finds it irritating when the Gentleman says it repeatedly on the telephone. At the end, Sarah uses exactly the same expression when she finally encounters him.

For your B1 German studies, this story is particularly useful for conversational expressions, indirect narration, and the German Perfekt tense. Read the original again after the English version, especially sections 3–5, to see how the author builds the misunderstanding.
